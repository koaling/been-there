## The council: launch consumer camera glasses at €1,400 now?

**Short verdict: No, not a broad consumer launch as things stand. Do a gated launch to the people who already love it, and make bystander privacy part of the hardware before you scale.** The testers' enthusiasm tells you the product works. The bar bans tell you society hasn't accepted it yet, and at €1,400 you can't afford to be the brand people associate with creeps.

---

### 1. The Product Optimist: "Ship it"
- Early testers loving it is rare and valuable. Most hardware never gets there.
- Every new camera form factor has been resisted at first: Kodak cameras in the 1890s, camera phones in the 2000s. People got used to them.
- If you wait, a competitor with a bigger marketing budget will define the category and set the norms.
- **Weak spot:** this voice is reading enthusiasm from self-selected testers. Those are the people who *chose* to wear the glasses, not the people being looked at.

### 2. The Bystander Advocate: "The real user is the person being filmed"
- The person wearing the glasses gets all the benefit. The people around them carry all the cost, and they never agreed to it. This is the Google Glass "Glasshole" problem, and it killed that product.
- With an "always-available" camera, people can't tell *whether* they're being recorded, and that uncertainty is enough to change how they behave. Bars aren't banning recording. They're banning the *ambiguity*.
- **Weak spot:** this voice tends to treat every recording as harmful, when most uses (hands-free photos, navigation, accessibility) are harmless.

### 3. The Regulatory & Legal Counsel: "In the EU, this isn't only a PR problem"
- **GDPR:** filming identifiable people in public can take a user outside the "purely personal or household" exemption (the CJEU's *Ryneš* case is the standard reference). Your company also has its own exposure if footage goes through your cloud, gets processed by AI, or is used for training. You'll want a DPIA, privacy by design and by default (Art. 25), and clear answers on where footage goes and how long it's kept.
- **EU AI Act:** if the glasses do any face recognition or identify people, you're in very sensitive territory. Biometric identification and untargeted collection of facial images face heavy restrictions or outright bans. Keep any identity features out of the product completely.
- **Spain specifically:** the right to one's own image (LO 1/1982) and the AEPD's track record of enforcing on cameras make Spain a market where a viral incident turns into a regulatory case.
- **Product liability and app stores:** expect retailers, venues, schools, gyms, hospitals and workplaces to set their own policies. Your product will be banned by default in a lot of places.
- **Weak spot:** none of this stops a launch. It sets conditions on one.

### 4. The Brand & Commercial Strategist: "€1,400 changes the maths"
- At this price you're selling to early adopters and professionals, not the mass market. The volume a "consumer launch" would bring you is small, and the reputational risk is large.
- One viral video ("guy secretly filming in a changing room / at a school gate with €1,400 glasses") will define your brand more than any campaign you run.
- Bar bans are a leading indicator. Today it's bars. Next it's gyms, concerts, offices and hospitals. Each ban takes away a use case and makes the product less worth its price.
- **What works better:** position it on the *uses* (hands-free capture for creators, accessibility for low-vision users, field work, travel), not "a camera on your face."

### 5. The Hardware/Engineering Realist: "Privacy has to be physical, not a setting"
- A software toggle won't convince bystanders. What will:
  - **A bright recording LED that can't be disabled**, with tamper detection: if the LED is covered or broken, the camera won't record.
  - **A physical camera shutter or detachable module** so the wearer can visibly show "off". This lets them stay welcome in bars.
  - **An audible or visible capture cue** for photos.
  - **On-device processing by default**, with no automatic cloud upload, and automatic face blurring on anything shared or uploaded.
  - **No facial recognition, ever**, and say so publicly.
- **Weak spot:** each of these adds cost and gets in the way of the "seamless" experience testers love. Expect some pushback from the optimist.

### 6. The Devil's Advocate: "Maybe 'always-available camera' is the wrong product"
- What do testers *actually* love? If it's the audio, the AI assistant, the display or the notifications, you may be carrying all the camera risk for a feature that isn't what drives purchases.
- Check the usage data: how often do testers actually capture? If the camera is a minor feature, a version with no camera (or a detachable one) could open up the mainstream market.

---

### Where the council agrees
- Product-market fit with **wearers** looks real.
- Social licence from **bystanders** does not exist yet, and the bans prove it.
- A broad consumer launch right now risks a "Google Glass moment" that would be very hard to recover from at this price.

### Where they disagree
- **Speed vs. trust:** the optimist fears losing first-mover advantage. Legal and brand think being first at shaping norms responsibly matters more than being first to ship.
- **How much friction to add:** engineering's privacy hardware vs. the seamless experience testers love.

### Recommended path
1. **Now: a gated launch.** Sell by invitation or waitlist to the early-adopter and creator segments, plus clear B2B/accessibility use cases. Small, monitored, with a feedback loop.
2. **Before scaling: build the privacy features into the hardware.** That means the tamper-proof LED, a physical shutter, on-device processing by default and no facial recognition. Have the DPIA done and published in summary.
3. **Go to venues before they ban you.** Offer a simple "camera-off mode" bars can ask for, and signage or partner programmes. Turn venues from opponents into allies.
4. **Measure social acceptance as a launch gate, not just user satisfaction.** Survey bystanders, track ban counts and media sentiment. Scale to the general public when those numbers move, not before.
5. **Look into the camera-optional SKU** in case the devil's advocate is right.

**Bottom line:** the product is ready for its fans. The world isn't ready for the product yet. Use the next 6 to 12 months to earn bystander trust through hardware and policy, because that, not the tech, is what will decide whether this becomes a category or a cautionary tale.
