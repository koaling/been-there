# Council: Shut down the game and turn the chat tool into the product?

There's a famous precedent here. **Slack** came out of Tiny Speck's failed game *Glitch*, and **Discord** came out of Hammer & Chisel's struggling game *Fates Forever*. So it has worked before. But for every Slack there are hundreds of internal tools that only their makers loved. Here's how five advisors would see it.

---

### 1. The Pivot Advocate: "Follow the pull, not the plan"
You can't force pull from a market. You've spent time pushing the game and it isn't working. The chat tool has something the game doesn't: people use it without being asked. That's the rarest signal in startups. Teams that pivot toward real usage beat teams that keep polishing what they planned. Holding on to the game out of sunk cost is the most common way founders burn their last year of runway.

**Vote:** Pivot, and soon.

### 2. The Skeptic: "Everyone who tries it" is your own team
Who has actually tried it? If it's mostly your own people, plus maybe friends or contractors, that's not validation. It's a tool built around your exact workflow, used by people who like you, who don't pay for it, and who never had to migrate off Slack or Teams to use it. Internal tools feel magical because they fit one team perfectly. That fit usually doesn't carry over.

Also, chat is one of the most crowded and entrenched categories in software. Slack, Teams, Discord and Google Chat are effectively free, built into suites, and protected by network effects. "Nicer chat" doesn't win. Slack itself only won because it arrived at a specific moment with a specific angle: searchable history plus integrations, replacing email.

**Vote:** Not yet. You don't have evidence, you have a feeling.

### 3. The Market Strategist: What exactly do people love?
The real question isn't "game or chat." It's **what is the one thing about this tool that people would miss?** Possible answers:
- A specific feature, such as threads done right, async updates, built-in game-dev workflows, or great search
- Speed or simplicity compared with bloated tools
- A niche fit, for example game studios, remote dev teams, or communities

If the answer is "it's just a really nice chat," that's a weak business. If it's "it solves X for teams like ours in a way Slack can't," that's a wedge. A very interesting possibility: **your game-dev background may be the wedge.** Discord won by focusing on gamers first, not "everyone."

**Vote:** Pivot only toward a sharp niche, never toward "a general chat app."

### 4. The CFO: How much runway do you have, and what does the game cost to keep alive?
This decides the timing more than anything else.
- **What does the game cost each month** in servers, live ops and support? Does it bring in any revenue?
- **How many months of runway** do you have left?
- **Do you have players or obligations** (purchases, subscriptions, a publisher deal, backers) that make shutting down messy?

If runway is under 9 to 12 months, you can't afford to run two products. If the game has a small but paying base, you could switch it to maintenance mode instead of killing it outright.

**Vote:** Depends on the numbers. Decide by the calendar, not by mood.

### 5. The Operator: Don't make a binary bet. Run a 4 to 6 week test first.
You don't have to choose today. Validate the chat tool with **outsiders** before you pull the plug:
1. **Give it to 5 to 10 outside teams** that have no relationship with you. Watch what happens without hand-holding.
2. **Measure real behavior:** Is it still used daily after 2 to 4 weeks? Do they invite more people? Do they complain when it breaks?
3. **Ask for money or commitment:** a letter of intent, a paid pilot, or even "would you be very disappointed if this disappeared?" Aim for 40% or more saying "very disappointed."
4. **Meanwhile, freeze the game.** No new features, only maintenance, so the team's energy goes into the test.

**Vote:** Test fast, then commit fully either way.

---

## Verdict

**Lean toward the pivot, but earn it with outside evidence first.** The signal you describe is real and worth chasing, and the history of game studios pivoting into communication tools is no accident. Game teams often build excellent real-time tools for themselves. Still, "our team loves it" is the weakest form of product validation, and chat is a brutal market if you go after it head-on.

**Recommended path:**
- **Now:** Put the game in maintenance mode and stop investing in it.
- **Next 4 to 6 weeks:** Run the chat tool with 5 to 10 external teams, focused on a specific niche.
- **Pre-set kill criteria:** For example: "If 3 or more external teams use it daily after 4 weeks and at least 1 will pay, we pivot fully. If not, we look for a third option, which may be a different product or selling the tech or team."
- **Then:** Commit 100%. Running half a game and half a chat product is the worst outcome.

## Questions that would change the advice
1. Who has tried the chat tool outside your company, if anyone?
2. What specifically do users say they love about it?
3. How much runway do you have, and does the game bring in any revenue?
4. Is there something about your team (game dev, real-time networking, a community) that gives you an unfair advantage in a specific niche?
