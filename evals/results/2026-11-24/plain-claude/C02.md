**Short answer: no, it's not too late. A crowded market isn't what should worry you. The real question is whether the waitlist reflects a pain that current products leave unsolved, or just a nicely made video.**

Fun fact: this is almost exactly Dropbox's origin story. In 2007 "backup and sync" was already crowded with names like Carbonite, Mozy, SugarSync, Microsoft's FolderShare and a dozen others. Drew Houston's demo video took the beta waitlist from about 5,000 to 75,000 overnight. They won anyway, because the others technically did sync but never actually "just worked." So history says yes, this can work. It also says the bar is very high.

Here's the council.

---

### The Optimist (Market)
- A crowded market is **proof of demand**, not proof of saturation. People already pay for this, so you don't have to teach them the category exists.
- The incumbents (Dropbox, Google Drive, OneDrive, iCloud) have grown into "work platforms" full of upsells, AI features and enterprise needs. Their sync core has often gotten worse: conflicts, "files on demand" that won't open offline, iCloud getting stuck. **Bloat opens a gap for a focused tool.**
- A big waitlist within days of a demo, with no product yet, is a strong signal. Most crowded-market launches get no reaction at all.

### The Skeptic (Waitlist)
- **A waitlist sign-up is the cheapest commitment there is.** One email costs nothing. Before you celebrate, find out:
  - Where did the traffic come from? A single viral post on HN or Reddit, or many different sources?
  - What share of sign-ups open your follow-up emails? Would they answer a 3-question survey?
  - **Would they pay?** Put in a pre-order or a paid founding plan and look at the conversion rate. If 1–3% of a cold waitlist pays, that's a real signal. If almost nobody does, it was curiosity.
- Demo videos lie by nature. They show the happy path, and sync is all edge cases.

### The Engineer (Feasibility)
- "Just works" is **the hardest promise in all of software**. Making sync work 95% of the time takes a few weeks. The last 5% takes years: conflicts, renames during sync, case-sensitive vs. case-insensitive filesystems, symlinks, extended attributes, huge files, millions of small files (`node_modules`!), flaky networks, sleeping laptops, clocks out of step, partial writes.
- **One data-loss bug destroys the brand for good.** Trust is your real product, more than features are.
- Ask yourself plainly: does the demo run on a real engine that holds up under those edge cases, or is it a prototype? The answer sets your real time to market.

### The Strategist (Positioning)
- You can't beat Dropbox, Google or Apple at being "everything for everyone." They bundle storage with the OS or with suites people already pay for (free in practice). **Don't compete on storage. Compete on the experience, or on a niche the giants ignore.** Some options:
  - **Cross-platform for people who mix ecosystems** (Mac + Windows + Linux), where iCloud and OneDrive fall short.
  - **Developers / creatives**: big projects, `.git` and `node_modules` handled intelligently, heavy media files (video, 3D, audio).
  - **Privacy / local-first / E2E encryption**: syncing between your own machines without your data living on someone else's cloud (Syncthing's territory, but with a UX normal people can use).
  - **Bring-your-own-storage**: sync on top of S3, B2, a NAS, so you don't sell GB at all.
- **The waitlist can tell you which niche is yours.** Segment the sign-ups (OS, profession, what they use today) before you choose.

### The CFO (Business)
- Storage is a commodity with shrinking margins, and the giants price it at a loss. **If your model is "€X per month for N GB," you lose.**
- Better models: charge for the software/experience (license or subscription with no storage, or BYO storage), per device, or for pro features (versioning, selective sync, sharing).
- Support and trust cost a lot. Every bug report is "I lost my files." Budget for that.

### The Devil's Advocate
- Distribution is the incumbents' real moat. iCloud and OneDrive come **preinstalled**. You're asking people to install something extra, give it access to their files, and trust it with their data. That's a lot of friction.
- Previous "Dropbox, but better" attempts (Bitcasa, Copy, Wuala, etc.) died, mostly on economics and trust, not technology.
- If the waitlist mostly came from a tech audience, that audience is enthusiastic, demanding, and not very willing to pay.

---

### Council verdict

**Not too late, but only with conditions.** Timing isn't the risk. These are:
1. **Turning interest into payment.** Validate it now.
2. **Reliability.** "Just works" has to be literally true, or the pitch hurts you.
3. **Positioning.** A clear niche, not a generic "better Dropbox."
4. **A model that isn't selling GB.**

### Next steps (this month)
1. **Segment the waitlist**: send a short survey (OS, what they use now, what frustrates them, how much they'd pay).
2. **Paid founding offer** (lifetime or first year at a discount) for the first X people. Measure conversion.
3. **Closed beta with 50–100 users** from the segment that responds best, with heavy telemetry on conflicts and errors. Treat any data loss as a stop-everything event.
4. **Write the one-line positioning**: "The sync for [specific segment] that [what the others don't do]." If you can't fill in the blanks, you aren't ready to launch yet.
