**Short answer: not like this.** Going big is fine. Going big before you know *why* each order loses €4 is how grocery delivery companies die. Here's the council.

---

### The CFO
"Volume will fix it" holds only if the €4 loss comes from **fixed costs** spread over too few orders: rent, idle staff, a warehouse running at 30%. If it comes from **variable costs** (rider or driver time per drop, picking labour, packaging, payment fees, discounts, small baskets), then more volume just means more loss. Eight cities at −€4 is eight times the burn.

Before anything else, split that €4 into:
- Contribution margin per order (gross margin on the basket minus picking, delivery, packaging, payments, promos)
- Fixed site costs per order at current volume
- What each of those looks like at 2x, 5x and 10x volume

If contribution margin per order is already negative, scale makes things worse.

### The operator
Automated warehouses are the worst asset to commit to before you know your demand. They are:
- **Capex-heavy and slow.** 12 to 24+ months to build and commission each one, and building 8 at once multiplies execution risk.
- **Only profitable at high utilization.** An automated site at 25% capacity is far more expensive per order than a manual dark store.
- **Hard to undo.** If a city disappoints, you can't move the robots cheaply.

You also haven't proven your operating playbook in one city yet. Replicating an unproven playbook 8 times replicates its flaws 8 times, all at once, with no room to learn.

### The historian
This exact move has been tried:
- **Webvan** (late 1990s) raised huge sums, built large automated warehouses in multiple US cities ahead of demand, and went bankrupt in 2001. The thesis was "scale will fix the unit economics."
- **The 2021–2022 quick-commerce wave** (Gorillas, Getir, Jokr, Fridge No More, Buyk and others) raised billions, expanded into many cities at once to "win the land grab," and then mostly collapsed, retreated or got sold off cheaply when funding tightened.
- **Ocado**, the automation success story, spent many years tuning one model before it scaled, and it now makes much of its money licensing that tech.

The winners proved density and unit economics first. The losers bought scale.

### The strategist (the "get ahead of competitors" argument)
Grocery delivery has **weak network effects and low switching costs**. Customers go wherever it's cheapest and most reliable this week. Being first in 8 cities doesn't lock anything in. You can lose a land grab to a better-capitalized player, or to a supermarket chain that already has stores and supplier terms. A head start that loses money buys you very little defensibility.

What actually is defensible: **density** (orders per km² per hour), **basket size**, **supplier margins**, and **operational excellence**. All of those get built city by city.

### The optimist (to be fair)
There are real arguments for moving fast:
- Capital is available now and might not be later.
- Some costs really are fixed (tech, brand, procurement), and more cities help spread them.
- Supplier terms improve with total purchasing volume.
- Automation can, in principle, cut picking cost per order sharply at high utilization.

None of these need 8 automated warehouses at once. You get most of the benefit from a staged rollout.

---

### Verdict: go big in stages, with gates
1. **Diagnose city 1 (4–8 weeks).** Break down the €4. Find the levers: minimum basket, delivery fees, delivery windows (same-day lets you batch, which is a big advantage over 15-minute delivery), SKU range, promo discipline.
2. **Get city 1 to positive contribution margin**, even if it's still negative after fixed costs. That proves volume *can* fix it.
3. **Expand to 2–3 cities using cheap, reversible infrastructure**: leased or manual micro-fulfillment, or partnering with existing retailers. Check that the playbook travels.
4. **Commit to automation where density is proven.** Build automated sites only in cities that hit agreed volume and utilization thresholds.
5. **Keep most of the round as runway.** In this sector, the company still standing in 3 years is usually the one that wins the market.

**Suggested gates for each expansion step:** positive contribution margin per order, a target orders/day per site, retention or repeat rate (for example monthly cohort retention), and customer acquisition cost paid back within an agreed number of months.
