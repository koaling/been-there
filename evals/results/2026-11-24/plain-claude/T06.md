**Short answer: no, not yet.** Right now each kitchen loses money on two basic costs: food you throw away and the cost of getting each meal to a customer. Opening three more cities copies those losses three more times. Scale only helps once each kitchen works on its own. Here's how five advisors see it.

---

### The CFO: "Scale multiplies your unit economics, good or bad"
- 20% waste means one in five meals you pay to make earns nothing. On a meal with a food cost of €4–5, that's roughly €1 lost on every meal you sell.
- Delivery cost depends on how many orders a driver can drop per hour in one area. It doesn't drop because you have more cities. Three new cities start with *fewer* orders per area than your current one, so delivery costs there will be **worse** at first, not better.
- Each new kitchen brings rent, equipment, staff and launch marketing before it gets any orders. You'd be spending a lot of cash on a model that isn't profitable yet.
- **Question to answer first:** after waste and delivery, what's your profit per order in your best-performing area today? If it's negative or close to zero, expanding won't fix it.

### The Operations lead: "Fix the waste first, it's the easiest win"
The waste happens because you cook before you know demand. You can attack that directly:
- **Order cutoffs:** customers order by e.g. 8pm the day before, or 10am for dinner. You cook what's been ordered plus a small buffer.
- **Subscriptions or meal plans:** weekly plans make demand predictable, and you also get the cash upfront.
- **Shorter menus that share ingredients:** fewer dishes built from the same base items means leftover stock can go into tomorrow's meals.
- **Cook in stages:** prepare components ahead and finish to order or in late batches.
- **Sell the leftovers:** discounted late-day sales (Too Good To Go and similar), staff meals, or B2B.
- A realistic target is single-digit waste. Going from 20% to 5–8% could be worth more than a new city.

### The Logistics lead: "Delivery is about how close together your orders are"
- **Delivery windows** instead of on-demand. Grouping orders onto fixed routes can cut the cost per drop sharply.
- **Pickup points** in offices, gyms and coworking spaces. One stop can cover 20–50 meals.
- **Corporate and B2B accounts:** daily office lunches give you volume at one address and steady demand. That fixes waste and delivery at the same time.
- **Minimum order or delivery fee** that rewards bigger or grouped orders.
- **Win one area completely** before spreading thinly over a whole city, let alone four.

### The Customer advocate: "Do people come back?"
- In daily meal delivery the business lives or dies on retention: how many customers are still ordering after 4, 8 and 12 weeks. If they are, they'll accept ordering ahead and delivery windows. If they aren't, more cities just means paying again and again to replace lost customers.
- Before expanding, check what share of revenue comes from repeat customers and how much it costs you to win one.

### The Devil's advocate: "Is there a case for expanding now?"
To be fair, there are reasons to expand:
- If a competitor is about to take those cities and getting there first really matters.
- If you've raised money specifically to expand and investors expect it.
- If your current city's market is simply too small to ever reach the density you need.

Even then, the smart move is **one** new city, run as a test with the fixed model. Not three at once.

---

### Verdict
**Pause the 3-city plan. Spend 3–6 months fixing the model in your current city, then expand against clear targets.**

Suggested targets to meet before opening a new kitchen:
| Metric | Target before expanding |
|---|---|
| Food waste | < 8% |
| Delivery cost per order | < 15–20% of the order value |
| Profit per order after delivery | Clearly positive in your best area |
| Customers still ordering after 8 weeks | Strong and stable (set your own target, e.g. 40%+) |
| Share of orders that are pre-ordered or subscription | > 60% |

Once you hit those numbers, each new kitchen is a copy of something that works, and expansion becomes a real plan instead of a bet.
