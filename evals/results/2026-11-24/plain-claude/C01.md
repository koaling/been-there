**Short answer: yes, keep going. Paying hosts and guests already answer the "nobody will do this" objection.** What you still need to find out is whether people pay *repeatedly*, and whether trust can work at scale. Here's the council.

---

### The Skeptic
"Nobody sleeps in a stranger's home" isn't fully wrong. *Most* people won't, at least at first. The real risks:
- **Safety and trust incidents.** One assault, theft or trashed apartment can sink you, in the press and in the law.
- **Regulation.** Cities, landlords, HOAs and hotel lobbies will push back on zoning, taxes and lease violations.
- **Event demand is spiky.** Sold-out conferences happen a few times a year per city. That's a wedge, not a business.
- **Supply quality.** Air mattresses and random spare rooms give a very uneven experience.

### The Optimist
Paying customers beat opinions. "Everyone says" usually means people imagining themselves as the user. Your users are a different group: budget-constrained, adventurous, or stuck because hotels are full. Couchsurfing and B&Bs already show some people will do this. Hosts have an unused asset and a real reason to earn from it. When a market looks absurd to most people, it's often underserved.

### The Customer Voice
- **Guests** want a cheap, available bed near the event, and they want to know they won't get scammed or hurt.
- **Hosts** want easy extra money, and they want to know the guest isn't dangerous and they'll get paid.
Neither side is buying "a stranger's home." Both are buying **trust plus convenience**. Build for that.

### The Operator
What turns "weird" into "normal":
1. **Identity and reputation.** Real profiles, verified IDs, two-way reviews after every stay.
2. **Payments held by the platform.** No cash changing hands. The host is paid after check-in.
3. **Host guarantee or insurance**, even a modest one at first.
4. **Great photos and listings.** Go take them yourselves if you have to. It increases trust and conversion.
5. **Hands-on support.** Personally talk to every early host and guest. Do things that don't scale.

### The Strategist
Events are a smart beachhead: urgent demand, a clear reason to try it, easy to market. But plan the path out early:
- Event city → same city when there's no event → other cities.
- Spare room / air mattress → whole homes and apartments (bigger tickets, less awkward).
- Event travelers → leisure travelers looking for something cheaper or more local.
The size of the opportunity sits in the expansion, not the wedge.

### The Numbers Person
Before you scale, track:
- **Repeat rate.** Do guests book again? Do hosts list again after their first stay?
- **Organic referrals.** Are people telling friends without being asked?
- **Incident rate.** Problems per 100 stays.
- **Unit economics.** Take rate × average booking value vs. what it costs to acquire a host and a guest.
- **Off-event demand.** Will anyone book in a week with no event?

---

### Verdict
**Keep going, but treat the next 2-3 months as a test of these hypotheses:**
1. People who tried it come back and refer others.
2. Trust tools (reviews, verification, payments, a guarantee) keep incidents rare.
3. Demand exists outside sold-out event weeks.

If those hold, the skeptics are describing a habit that hasn't formed yet, not a lack of demand. If retention is flat and nobody books outside events, you have a seasonal niche, and you should decide whether that's enough for you.

**Concrete next step:** pick one upcoming event in one city. Personally recruit 20-30 hosts and photograph their places yourselves. Run every stay hands-on, then measure repeat bookings and reviews. That's your evidence for (or against) the bigger bet.
