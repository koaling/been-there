**Short verdict: don't launch at this price and cost structure.** Everyone on the council below agrees that the unit economics fail, though they give different reasons. Launching this way "grabs the market" only in the sense that you pay for each customer you win.

---

## The Council

### 1. The CFO: "The math breaks at the second visit"

- Revenue per subscriber: €9.99 a month. In Spain the price includes VAT, so at 21% you actually keep about **€8.26**, and then payment fees come out.
- Cost per visit: about €9.
- **Break-even is less than one film a month.** A subscriber who goes once already costs you money.

| Visits/month | Cost | Margin per sub |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | €0 | +€8.26 |
| 1 | €9 | –€0.74 |
| 2 | €18 | –€9.74 |
| 4 | €36 | –€27.74 |
| 8 | €72 | –€63.74 |

Take a realistic mix: 40% of subscribers go about 0.5 times a month, 30% go about 2.5 times, and 30% go about 6 times. That averages **2.75 visits**, or about €25 in cost against €8 of revenue. You lose roughly **€16–17 per subscriber per month**, and the loss grows with every subscriber you add.

### 2. The Behavioural Economist: "The gym analogy is wrong"

Gyms make money from light users for two reasons:
- **The marginal cost of a visit is close to zero.** The gym has already paid for the building and the machines, so an extra visit costs it almost nothing. You pay €9 cash for every visit.
- **People buy gym memberships out of aspiration** ("I'll go 3 times a week"). Nobody buys an unlimited cinema pass to *become* someone who goes to the cinema.

There's also **adverse selection**. Someone who goes to the cinema once a month can just buy a ticket. The people who see an "unlimited" plan as obviously great value are the heavy users, and those are exactly the customers who sink you. Your marketing will attract them first.

### 3. The Historian: "This has been tried"

MoviePass (USA, 2017–2019) used almost exactly this model: about $9.95 a month for unlimited films, paying cinemas full price. It grew to millions of subscribers very quickly. Then it collapsed. It added limits, blocked films and changed terms, users revolted, and the company went bankrupt. The models that survived look different:
- **Chains run their own subscriptions** (Cineworld Unlimited, AMC Stubs A-List, Pathé/UGC passes). Their marginal cost is an empty seat, and they make their margin on popcorn and drinks.
- **Capped plans** (e.g. 3 films a month) priced well above the cost of one ticket.

### 4. The Cinema Partner: "Why would I give you a discount if you're paying full price?"

At the moment you pay full price, so the cinema chains have no reason to work with you and every reason to copy you. Your real leverage is something else: **you fill empty seats on Monday to Thursday afternoons and evenings, and those viewers buy concessions.** A cinema should accept a wholesale price of something like **€4–5 per visit** for that, especially off-peak. Without a negotiated cost, you don't have a business.

### 5. The Growth Strategist (devil's advocate): "But land-grab..."

The best case for your plan goes like this: burn money to win subscribers, become the gatekeeper for audiences, then use that leverage to negotiate with cinemas and raise prices.

The problems with that:
- Every new subscriber makes your loss *bigger*, so you have no economies of scale. The faster you grow, the faster you die.
- Your customers come for the price, not for you. When you raise prices or add caps, they leave (MoviePass again).
- The cinema chains can launch their own pass at a lower cost than yours at any time.

A land-grab can work when costs fall as you grow. Here they don't.

---

## What the council would launch instead

1. **Negotiate the cost of a visit first.** Don't launch until you have wholesale deals (€4–5 a visit, or revenue share on off-peak sessions).
2. **Cap the plans:**
   - €9.99: 2 films a month, off-peak only (Monday to Thursday)
   - €17.99: 4 films a month, any time
   - €24.99+: unlimited, only once your wholesale cost makes it viable
3. **Make money on extras:** commission on concessions, premium formats (IMAX/VOSE) as add-ons, partner offers (dinner plus film).
4. **Test with a cohort before scaling:** 500–1,000 users in one city (Pamplona works) for 3 months. Measure how many visits each user makes and how many cancel before you spend on growth.
5. **Kill metric:** if the average subscriber makes more than (net price ÷ cost per visit) visits a month, you lose money, so stop or change the pricing.

**Bottom line:** the idea of a subscription that fills empty cinema seats can work. A €9.99 unlimited plan that pays full ticket price can't. Fix your cost per visit before you think about growth.
