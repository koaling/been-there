Short answer: **no.** If each order loses money, more orders means bigger losses. This plan is very close to Pets.com (2000): heavy, cheap products, free shipping, prices below cost, a famous ad campaign, and bankruptcy about 9 months after its IPO. Here is how five advisors see it.

---

## The Council

### 1. The CFO: "Volume multiplies, it doesn't fix"
Volume only helps when your **variable** cost per order falls below what you earn per order. Shipping is mostly a variable cost: every 15 kg bag needs its own truck space, handling and last-mile delivery. Volume gets you better carrier rates, maybe 10–25% off. It doesn't turn a negative margin positive.

A quick example (illustrative numbers):

| Per 15 kg bag | Amount |
|---|---|
| Selling price (below supermarket) | €38 |
| Cost of goods | €32 |
| Gross margin | **€6** |
| Shipping + packing + handling | €9–12 |
| Payment fees (~1.5–2%) | €0.70 |
| **Contribution per order** | **–€4 to –€7** |

Sell 100,000 bags and you lose €400k–700k **before** paying for the TV campaign, salaries or the warehouse. The ads just make you lose money faster.

### 2. The Logistics lead: "You picked the hardest category to ship"
Dog food and cat litter are heavy, bulky, cheap per kilo and bought again and again. That's the worst mix for parcel delivery. The companies that make money on them (Chewy, Zooplus, Tiendanimal) get there with:
- **Their own or dedicated fulfilment networks**, with high density per route
- **Free-shipping thresholds** (for example, free over €49 or €69), so one delivery covers several items
- **Larger baskets**: food plus treats, toys and pharmacy items, which carry the real margin
- Years of losses, funded by a lot of capital

Your scale discounts will arrive late. Your losses start with the first order.

### 3. The Marketing lead: "TV brings the wrong customers at the wrong moment"
- TV is costly and hard to measure. Spending big before your unit economics work pays to grow a loss.
- Customers who come for "cheaper than the supermarket" leave for whoever is cheaper next. Their loyalty is weak.
- Supermarkets and Amazon can match your price whenever they want. You can't beat them on cost of goods.
- Test with small, measurable channels first (search, local social, vets and groomers). Learn your real CAC and repeat rate before spending on TV.

### 4. The Customer's voice: "What I actually want"
Pet owners don't mainly want "€1 cheaper." They want **never to carry a 15 kg bag again and never to run out**. That's a convenience problem, and you can charge for convenience:
- Automatic subscription deliveries (predictable demand lets you plan routes)
- A reminder before the bag runs out
- Help choosing food by breed, age and health

People will pay supermarket price, or slightly more, for that.

### 5. The Contrarian: "When could volume work?"
To be fair, volume can help in some cases:
- If you reach **route density** (many deliveries per street), last-mile cost per order drops a lot. That needs local concentration, not national TV.
- If volume gets you **much better purchase prices** from manufacturers or lets you sell a **private label** at 30–40% margin.
- If losses are a **planned, capped** acquisition cost, and the data shows customers come back for 12+ months and later buy higher-margin items.

Without those, "volume will fix it" is a hope, not a plan.

---

## Verdict
**Don't launch it as described.** Fix the per-order economics first, then grow.

## What to do instead
1. **Calculate the true contribution margin per order** (product, shipping, packing, payment, returns). It has to be positive before you spend on ads.
2. **Set a free-shipping threshold or a minimum order** instead of shipping everything free.
3. **Lead with subscription** (for example, 5% off for recurring orders) to raise retention and make routes predictable.
4. **Raise basket margin** with treats, accessories, supplements and a private label.
5. **Start in one region** (one city or metro area) to build delivery density. Consider your own van routes or local partners rather than parcel carriers.
6. **Compete on convenience, not lowest price.** Match supermarket prices at most; don't go below.
7. **Delay TV** until CAC, retention and LTV are proven in cheaper channels. Then use TV to scale something that already works.

One test to apply: *"If we stopped all marketing tomorrow, would each existing order make money?"* If not, more volume will only make the losses bigger.
