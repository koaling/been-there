**The council's verdict: no. Don't put the budget into launch content.** All five members agree. If you split the money roughly 25/75, you can still get a strong launch and keep most of the budget for when you know what works.

This idea has already been tested at full scale. Quibi launched in 2020 with about $1.75B, premium short episodes (under 10 minutes) from big-name directors and stars, a mobile-only, "watch on the go" pitch and a $5–8/month price. It shut down after about 6 months. Your plan is almost exactly the same, so the council starts from that.

---

**1. The Skeptic (market reality)**
"Premium" isn't what people want from short video on their phones. They want free, endless, social, and personal. A famous director's name helps in a film's marketing, but at 7 minutes on a 6-inch screen, viewers can't really see the difference that would justify paying. You'd be up against TikTok, Reels and Shorts, which cost nothing, plus Netflix and YouTube, which already sit on the same phone. Commuting is also a weak anchor: Quibi launched into lockdown, but even without that, commute time is already taken by music, podcasts and social apps.

**2. The Investor (capital allocation)**
Spending most of the budget on launch content means making your biggest bet when you know the least. You don't yet know which formats, genres, episode lengths or directors keep people subscribed. Content is a sunk cost, and if the first slate misses, nothing is left to adjust with. Subscription businesses live or die on retention and customer acquisition cost (CAC), not on how strong launch day is. Quibi's problem wasn't too little content. It had plenty and still lost 90%+ of its free-trial users.

**3. The Product Lead (distribution and format)**
The bottlenecks are distribution and social sharing, not content quality. Short video spreads through clips, shares and memes. A closed, paid app takes away that engine. Quibi blocked screenshots and sharing at first, and its content got almost no cultural buzz. If you keep going, the questions to answer are these. How does an episode get discovered by someone who isn't a subscriber yet? How can people share it? Where does it live outside your app (YouTube trailers, TikTok clips, TV casting)?

**4. The Creative Advocate (the case for the idea)**
There's something real here. Top directors working in tight formats can make memorable work, as short-film anthologies and premium ads show. The strongest version isn't a me-too Netflix for phones. It's a *curated, collectible* brand: a "Criterion of short form" or "The New Yorker of video." That means fewer, better works, a clear editorial identity and events around releases. That positioning can support a price, but it's a niche, not a mass market.

**5. The Operator (what to actually do)**
Stage the bet:
- **Phase 1 (10–20% of budget):** Commission 6–10 pieces. Release some free on YouTube, TikTok and Instagram to test demand, and measure completion rates, shares and email sign-ups.
- **Phase 2:** Test willingness to pay with a small paid tier, a festival-style "season pass", or B2B/licensing (airlines, trains, telecom bundles, brand-funded series).
- **Phase 3:** Put the bulk of the budget only into the formats and creators that showed real retention.
- Look at **partnerships** instead of a standalone app. Bundling with a telecom operator or streaming platform solves the distribution problem Quibi never cracked.

---

**Bottom line:** A big content budget is an advantage only if you know what to spend it on. Today you don't, so use a small part of it to find out. Before spending more, answer the hard question: **why would someone pay €5/month for this when the phone is already full of free and paid video?** If the honest answer is "because the directors are famous", Quibi has already tested that answer, and it failed.
