**Short answer: yes, it's a problem. It's probably the most important finding you have. Don't ship as-is.**

This is almost exactly what happened to Juicero in 2017. They had a $400–700 Wi-Fi press, sealed packs, QR freshness checks and around $120M from investors. Then a Bloomberg video showed people squeezing the packs by hand and getting roughly the same juice. The company shut down within months. The press didn't kill it. The story did: "a €600 machine that does what your hands do." Your testers have already found that story for you.

Here's how different perspectives on a council would see it:

---

**The customer**
"Why did I pay €600?" Once someone squeezes a pack by hand, they can't forget it. The pitch stops being "fresh juice, made easy" and turns into "a subscription with a pointless gadget attached." Expect bad reviews, refund requests and a lot of resentment.

**The press / social media**
This is a ready-made viral video. Somebody will film the hand-squeeze next to the machine. It's cheap to make, easy to understand and funny. You can't control that story after launch. You can only prevent it before.

**The investor (being honest)**
Investors like the hardware because hardware looks defensible. But if the hardware isn't needed, the defensibility is fake. Their money is safer if you deal with this now than if it blows up publicly after a big production run. A good investor would want to hear about this finding. If you hide it from them, you have a much worse problem.

**The engineer**
Ask what the press does that hands can't:
- Does it get measurably more juice out (yield %)?
- Is it more consistent, cleaner or faster?
- Is it more hygienic (no handling)?
- Is the QR check doing real work, or could a phone app do it?

If the honest answer is "about 5–10% more juice," that isn't worth €600. A real advantage would be 30%+ more yield, or something hands physically can't do.

**The business model strategist**
Your real product is probably the **packs**: the supply chain, the freshness, the subscription. That's where the recurring revenue and the moat are. The device is a way to get customers in, and right now it's an expensive one. Options:
1. **Make the device earn its price.** Redesign the packs so they really need the press (for example, whole or chopped produce that hands can't cold-press well). That's hard and expensive, and it only works if it's honest engineering. Making packs artificially "hand-proof" just to protect the device will backfire if anyone finds out.
2. **Make the device cheap or free.** Bundle it with a subscription (the razor-and-blades model). Then "you can squeeze it by hand" stops mattering.
3. **Drop the device and sell the packs.** Fresh, verified, delivered cold-pressed juice. Simpler, cheaper and much lower risk.
4. **Change what the device is for.** Position it as convenience or a design object at a much lower price, and openly say the packs work without it.

**The devil's advocate (for shipping)**
"Most people won't try squeezing by hand. Nespresso works." The difference is that you can't hand-brew a Nespresso pod and get the same espresso. Your testers already found the workaround without being asked. It's going to come out.

---

**My recommendation**
1. **Don't ship at €600 with the current packs.**
2. Run a proper side-by-side test this week: press vs. hand on yield, taste, time and mess, with numbers.
3. If the press doesn't win clearly, change course. Either make the hardware cheap or bundled, or lead with the packs.
4. Tell your investors now, along with a plan. "We found a Juicero-style risk in testing and here's how we're fixing it" makes you look strong. Having the same thing come out after launch is how companies go under.
