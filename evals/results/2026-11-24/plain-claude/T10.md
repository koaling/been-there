**The council's answer: don't proceed as described.** Owning the whole chain might fix construction one day. But your evidence so far points the wrong way. You plan to scale a system that hasn't yet delivered one project on time and on budget.

There's also a close precedent you should study first. **Katerra** raised about $2B+ (mostly from SoftBank) to do almost exactly this: design, factories, materials supply and general contracting, all at once and across many projects. It went bankrupt in 2021. Big funding didn't save it. It let Katerra scale its problems faster.

## The council

**1. The Operator (has run construction companies)**
> "Late and over budget on your *first* projects is the signal. Integration doesn't fix execution. It hides execution problems inside your own P&L, where nobody pushes back. With subcontractors, a bad trade gets fired. When it's your own factory, you keep feeding it work."

Main point: vertical integration multiplies whatever competence you already have. If that competence is low, you get more of the problem.

**2. The Factory Economist**
> "A factory is a fixed-cost machine. It only pays off with steady, repetitive volume of *standardized* products. Construction demand is lumpy, local, and every project is different: different sites, codes, clients, architects. If you design custom per project, your factory becomes a very expensive job shop."

Main point: you need a small, locked product catalogue and a guaranteed pipeline **before** building factories, not after.

**3. The Skeptical Investor**
> "Large funding is a risk here, not an advantage. It lets you start too many projects, build factories ahead of demand, and buy suppliers before you understand them. Ask: what does unit economics look like on *one* project, fully loaded? If you can't answer, you're not ready for many."

Main point: capital lets you postpone hard truths. Katerra's problems built up across dozens of parallel projects before anyone could see them in the numbers.

**4. The Systems Thinker (the case for)**
> "The thesis isn't crazy. Construction's fragmentation *is* a real cause of waste: misaligned incentives, no learning between projects, rework at handoffs. Integrated players can win: Toyota Home and Sekisui House in Japan, some Nordic volumetric builders. But they got there over decades, in narrow product types, with stable domestic demand."

Main point: the idea can work, but only with a narrow scope, standardized products and patience. Your plan has none of the three.

**5. The Risk Officer**
> "Owning everything means owning every failure mode at once: factory ramp-up, logistics, on-site tolerances, permitting, supplier pricing, labor. Each one is a company-sized problem. Running many projects in parallel means you can't isolate which part is broken."

Main point: you lose the ability to diagnose. Right now you can't tell whether your overruns come from design, factory, supply, site or management. Integrating everything makes that even harder to see.

## Points of disagreement

- **Is integration itself wrong?** The Systems Thinker says no, only the sequencing is. The Operator and Investor think integration is premature until you've shown you can execute.
- **Speed vs. proof:** with big funding, there's pressure to grab the market fast. Most of the council thinks speed is exactly what killed earlier attempts.

## Verdict: don't proceed as planned. Change the order.

1. **Post-mortem the first projects honestly.** Find exactly where time and money leaked: design changes, factory defects, site rework, supply delays, permits. If you can't name the top 3 causes with numbers, stop here.
2. **Narrow the product.** Pick one building type (e.g., mid-rise residential, or one modular system) and standardize it hard. No custom designs.
3. **Integrate only the bottleneck.** Bring in-house only the parts of the chain that caused your overruns. Keep buying everything else from the market for now.
4. **Prove it on a few projects, run in sequence.** Get 3–5 projects in a row on time and on budget, with cost per m² falling as you go. That learning curve is your proof.
5. **Then scale** factories and geography, tied to a contracted pipeline, not a forecast.
6. **Set kill criteria now.** Example: "If project N+3 is still more than 10% over budget, we stop expansion."

**One-sentence version:** owning the whole chain only helps once you know how to run each link, and your first projects show you don't yet. Use the funding to buy that proof slowly, not to scale a model that hasn't worked yet.
