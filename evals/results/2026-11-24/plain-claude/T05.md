## The Council: should you double the discount budget?

**Short verdict: No. Not yet.** Your problem isn't growth speed. You have a leaky bucket, and doubling the discount means pouring money into it twice as fast. Here's how each advisor sees it.

---

### 1. The CFO: "Every new customer loses you money"

A rough unit-economics sketch (swap in your real numbers):

- Customer pays: **€19**
- Cleaner gets paid (say ~70% of €80): **~€55**
- **Loss on the first cleaning: ~€36**, plus marketing spend to acquire the customer (often €20–50)

So each new customer costs roughly **€55–85** before you make a cent. That's fine if they stay and book again. But you said many of them **book once and then hire the cleaner directly**. For those customers, lifetime value is about €0, and you never get the money back.

Doubling the budget doubles the losses. It doesn't fix the ratio.

### 2. The Marketplace Strategist: "Your real problem is disintermediation"

This is the central issue. Your platform introduces a customer to a cleaner, and after that the two of them don't need you anymore. Home cleaning is the textbook case for this:

- It's recurring, local and personal, with the same person in the same house every week.
- Once trust exists, the platform's commission (often 20–30%) looks like a tax to both sides.
- Paying the cleaner directly is cheaper for the customer and pays the cleaner more. They both win by leaving you.

In practice your discount pays for the introduction, and then the platform gets cut out. Homejoy in the US ran almost exactly this playbook, with cheap first cleanings, aggressive growth spending and freelance cleaners. It shut down in 2015, partly because of poor retention and leakage, and partly because of worker-classification lawsuits.

### 3. The Growth Lead: "Discounts attract the wrong customers"

A €19 cleaning (a 76% discount) mainly attracts **deal-seekers**, not people who want a cleaner every week. You're probably collecting:

- People who want one deep clean before a move or a visit
- People who hop from one promo to the next
- People who will happily hire the cleaner off-platform

Doubling the budget usually brings in a **worse cohort**: you've already reached the most motivated buyers, so the extra spend goes to less valuable ones.

### 4. The Cleaner's Advocate: "Ask why cleaners leave with your customers"

Cleaners leave with your customers because the platform gives them nothing beyond the first introduction. Think about what would make them want to stay:

- Steady hours and fast payment (for example, paid within 24h)
- Insurance against damage or injury
- No hassle chasing payments, handling cancellations or no-shows
- A lower commission on repeat customers, so leaving isn't worth it

If the platform only takes from cleaners, you can't build loyalty with them.

### 5. The Legal / Risk Advisor: "Freelance cleaners carry regulatory risk"

If you're operating in Spain or elsewhere in the EU, rules on platform work are tightening (the Ley Rider in Spain, the EU Platform Work Directive). If you control prices, assignments and quality, regulators may decide your "freelancers" are really employees. Scaling fast on a model that might get reclassified means **scaling a liability**. Check this before you grow, not after.

### 6. The Contrarian: "Maybe you're right to grow, just not this way"

Speed does matter in local marketplaces, because liquidity (enough cleaners and customers in one area) is a moat. So growth isn't wrong. But **buying one-off bookings doesn't build liquidity**. Recurring relationships that stay on the platform do.

---

### What the council recommends instead

**1. Measure before you spend.** Get these numbers by monthly cohort:
- % of customers who make a **2nd and 3rd booking on the platform**
- Contribution margin per cleaning after the first one
- CAC payback period (how many months until a customer covers what it cost to acquire them)

If fewer than about 30–40% come back for a second booking, more discounts will only make things worse.

**2. Tie the discount to recurrence instead of a single cleaning.**
- Swap "€19 first cleaning" for something like "**first cleaning €39 if you sign up for a fortnightly plan**" or "**4 cleanings for the price of 3**".
- The subsidy then pays for the habit, not just the introduction.

**3. Make staying on the platform worth more than leaving, for both sides.**
- *Customers:* satisfaction guarantee, insurance, easy rescheduling, a backup cleaner when theirs is sick, invoicing, automatic payment.
- *Cleaners:* lower commission for loyal clients, guaranteed minimum hours, instant payout, insurance, a reputation profile.
- A subscription model works especially well here: customers pay monthly and the platform handles everything.

**4. Add some friction to leaving (it helps, but it won't solve the problem on its own).**
- Hide contact details until a booking is made, keep messaging inside the app, and add a non-solicitation clause in the cleaner agreement with a reasonable "buy-out" fee. Each of these helps a little, but value is what actually keeps people.

**5. Then scale.** Once your repeat rate and payback period are healthy, increase spend in the zones where you already have density, not everywhere at once.

---

**Bottom line:** Doubling the discount speeds up the spending, not the growth. Fix retention and leakage first, then put money into acquisition. If you share your real numbers (cleaner payout, CAC, repeat rate), I can build the unit-economics model with you and find the break-even point.
