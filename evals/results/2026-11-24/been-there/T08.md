**Frame.** Decide whether to compete head-on with the market-leading music streamer on design and social features alone, at the same catalogue and price, given a marketing budget that is a fraction of the leader's.

- **Reversibility**: mostly a one-way door. Licensing the "same catalogue" means label deals, which usually come with advances or minimum guarantees, and a consumer launch spends money and runs on a clock you can't get back. Prototypes and tests before you sign are a two-way door.
- **Stakes**: money (licensing commitments plus burn), time (12–24 months), reputation with labels.
- **Horizon**: now, before you commit to licensing or a launch budget.

**Assumptions** (you didn't give numbers, so I'm going on these): the leader is Spotify or a player like it, with a free tier, years of each user's listening history and a large social graph. You're early stage and haven't launched or signed full catalogue deals yet. "Social features" means things like shared listening, following friends and collaborative playlists.

## Precedents

From the library:
- ☠ **Quibi** (2018–2020): died of having no market need and losing to competitors that were already in place. It launched premium mobile streaming into a crowded market against free alternatives, and its founders' shutdown letter said the idea may not have been strong enough to justify a standalone service. **Matches**: market (streaming media), business model (B2C subscription), mechanism (a "better" product with no reason to switch). **Differs**: Quibi had a huge budget ($1.75B) and new content, and you have neither. That is worse for you: even $1.75B couldn't buy a habit.
- ☠ **MoviePass** (2017–2019): died of unit economics. It paid suppliers full price for the content it resold. **Matches**: business model (entertainment subscription), mechanism (content costs set by suppliers, so your margin is out of your hands). **Differs**: MoviePass priced far below cost and you price at market. Use it only as a warning about margins.
- ✓ **AMC Stubs A-List**: survived the same subscription trap because **it controlled the cost of what it resold**. **Differs** from you: you won't own the content. The lesson is that subscriptions work when there is a structural advantage, not a cosmetic one.

From general knowledge (not in the library, so verify before relying on them; I've kept only facts I'm confident about):
- ☠ **Rdio** (2010–2015): the closest analogue. It was widely praised for its design and social features, had the same licensed catalogue as competitors at a similar price, and was outspent and out-distributed by Spotify. It filed for Chapter 11 in 2015 and Pandora bought its assets. **Matches**: market, model, timing (a leader already in place), mechanism (distribution). Matches all four dimensions.
- ☠ **Apple Ping** (2010–2012): a social layer for music that Apple shut down despite a huge installed base. **Matches**: mechanism (social features in music not driving behaviour), market. **Differs**: it was a feature, not a standalone service. This shows that even unlimited distribution didn't make music-social sticky.
- ✓ **Deezer**: survived Spotify largely through **distribution partnerships**, above all bundling with the telecom operator Orange in France, rather than through product superiority or ad spend. It found a channel where it didn't have to outspend the leader.

## Independent opinions

**1. Devil's advocate: against.** Rdio is your future. It had the better design, the social features and the same catalogue, and it still lost. In streaming, the product is the catalogue, and yours is identical. Design is the one advantage the leader can copy in a quarter. Social features suffer worst from your size: they are worth more the more friends are on the platform, and your users' friends are on the leader. The most fragile assumption is that people switch for a nicer interface when switching costs them their playlists, their listening history and recommendations tuned to them. *What would have to be true*: users would have to switch, and bring friends, without paid acquisition. **Confidence: high.**

**2. First principles: depends.** You're asking whether better design is enough, but the real problem is: why would anyone migrate their musical life? Third option: **don't be a full streamer.** Build the social layer *on top of* the leader and other services (shared listening, group playlists, taste-matching) using whatever integrations exist, with no licensing costs. Prove that the social behaviour is real and viral first. Then decide whether to license the catalogue, or sell or partner. Caveat: depending on a platform you build on is a real risk, because it can cut your access. *What would have to be true*: the social behaviour would have to be valuable on its own. Ping suggests it might not be. **Confidence: medium.**

**3. Expansionist: for, with a wedge.** You're not Rdio if you don't fight Rdio's war. Rdio fought for the general mass market. Deezer survived by owning a channel. The upside nobody sees: social features *are* distribution if they're built as loops (shared listening sessions, links that work for non-users, group playlists for parties or gyms). A narrow community where music is inherently shared (students, a scene, a region, a language market) can make the network effect work at small scale. *What would have to be true*: you'd need a referral or invite rate that cuts acquisition cost sharply, plus one segment where you're clearly the default. **Confidence: medium.**

**4. Outsider: against as described.** As a customer I'd hear: "it's Spotify, but prettier, for the same money." Then I'd ask what happens to my playlists, and whether my friends are there. If the answers are "you'll have to move them" and "no", I'm not switching. I might download it, admire it and go back. "Nicer design" isn't something I can explain to a friend in one sentence. "Let's listen to the same thing together right now" is. **(opinion)** **Confidence: medium.**

**5. Executor: depends.** Tomorrow at 9:00, the blocker isn't design. It's that **you can't launch "the same catalogue" without label licensing**, which takes long negotiations and often money up front. A small team with a small budget may wait months for deals. What you *can* do tomorrow: a clickable prototype and a social-feature test with 30–50 target users, plus a playlist-import tool, because migration friction is the first thing that will block you. **Confidence: high.**

**6. Finance (specialist, because licensing costs decide whether a same-price streamer can survive).** Against the plan as stated. Rights holders take most of streaming revenue. Treat the exact share as an estimate to verify with your licensing counsel, but it is commonly described as well over half. At the same price you get roughly the leader's gross margin, without its scale or its leverage with labels. That leaves little room to pay for users with ads, so a marketing gap can't be closed with spend. MoviePass shows what happens when suppliers set your costs. *What would have to be true*: near-zero paid acquisition (organic or partner channels) and label deals without heavy guarantees. **Confidence: medium-high.**

**7. Customer (specialist, because the whole bet rests on why people switch).** Depends. People rarely leave a streaming service over the interface. They leave over price, catalogue gaps, bundles (phone plan, device, other subscriptions) or because their group moves. Your best shot is the "group moves" mechanism. That means targeting groups, not individuals: friend groups, clubs, classes, households. **(opinion, consistent with the Deezer bundle precedent)** **Confidence: medium.**

## Pre-mortem

It's November 2027 and it failed. Why?

1. **People downloaded it, liked it and didn't stay.** *Warning sign*: high install-to-signup, but week-4 retention among users who still keep a leader subscription well below that of single-service users. That is the Rdio pattern.
2. **The leader copied the best social feature.** *Warning sign*: the leader ships shared sessions or collaborative features in your area within a few months of your launch, or already has them, and your differentiation story shrinks to "prettier".
3. **Licensing costs plus acquisition spend outran revenue.** *Warning sign*: paid cost per user above the gross margin a user brings in over 12 months, or label minimum guarantees that need a subscriber count you can't reach organically. This is the MoviePass mechanism.

## Chair's verdict

**Decision**: Compete with the market leader on design and social features alone, at the same catalogue and price, with a much smaller marketing budget.

**Verdict**: **Don't, as described.** Better design is not enough: it is the most copyable advantage you have, and the same catalogue and price leave nothing else to switch for. **First find out** whether your social features are a distribution loop for one specific group, before you sign licensing commitments. · Confidence: 7/10

**Agreement**: Every seat agrees that design is not a moat in a market where the catalogue is identical, switching costs (playlists, history, personalisation, friends) favour the incumbent, and you can't outspend the leader.

**Real disagreement**: Whether social features can replace marketing spend. Expansionist and Customer say yes, if you target groups. Devil's advocate cites Ping and the cold-start problem.

**Assumption that matters most**: Groups of friends will move *together* because of a social feature, and will bring in others without you paying for them. → **Cheapest test**: a prototype or a layer built on existing services, run with 5–10 real friend groups in one segment for 4 weeks. Measure invites sent per user, how many invited people activate, and weekly group usage. No licensing needed.

**Closest precedent**: **Rdio**. It matches on market, model, timing and mechanism. You are Rdio unless you bring something Rdio didn't have: a distribution channel (like Deezer's telco bundle) or a social loop that measurably brings in users.

**Next step (48 h)**: Write down your single sharpest social use case (for example, live group listening for a defined community) and the one segment where you'd be the default. Recruit 5 friend groups from that segment to a prototype test. In parallel, ask 2 possible distribution partners (telcos, device makers, venues, campus networks) whether they'd bundle or co-promote.

**Kill criterion**: If by the end of a 4-week test (target: early February 2027) fewer than half of the test groups are still using the social feature weekly, and each active user isn't bringing in at least ~0.3 new active users (an estimate, so adjust to your cost model), then don't license a catalogue to compete head-on. Pivot to the social layer, a niche, or a partnership.
