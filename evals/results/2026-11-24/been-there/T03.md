**Frame**: Decide whether to ship a €600 Wi-Fi press for your sealed juice packs now, or rework the product or offer first, given that test users can squeeze the packs by hand and get almost the same juice.

- **Reversibility**: one-way door. Once you commit to a production run, a €600 retail price and a public launch, it is hard to undo, and the "you don't need the machine" story can only break once.
- **Stakes**: money (tooling, inventory, the funding runway), reputation (a press story could define the brand), investor trust.
- **Horizon**: before you commit to manufacturing or a launch date.

**Assumptions** (no clarifying questions in this run): there's no production order yet; revenue is meant to come from the device plus recurring packs; "almost the same juice" comes from informal tests, not a measured comparison; the QR code checks expiry and/or authenticity.

## Precedents

☠ **Juicero (2013–2017)**. It died of overengineering, no real need for the machine and pricing. It sold an internet-connected press that worked only with its own packs, launched at $699 and cut to $400. In April 2017 Bloomberg showed the packs could be squeezed by hand with nearly the same result, and the company shut down in September 2017 after raising about $120M. **Matches: market, business model, failure mechanism. This is close to a 1:1 match.** Differs on timing: you found the flaw in testing, before launch, while Juicero's became public through the press. Juicero's packs also reportedly carried a QR code that the machine checked for freshness. That detail comes from general reporting, not the precedent library.

☠ **Keurig 2.0 (2014–2015)**: a failed product launch, not a dead company. It added a check on pod lids so only licensed pods would brew. Users saw it as a lock-in, workarounds spread, and Keurig publicly admitted the mistake and brought back its reusable filter. **Matches: market (kitchen appliance), business model (machine plus consumables), mechanism (a verification feature that users read as DRM, which hurt trust).** Differs: Keurig's machine really did make the coffee. *Source: well-known public reporting, not the precedent library; this entry would need sourcing.*

✓ **Nespresso**: survived the same "is the value in the machine or the capsule?" trap. The machine is physically necessary to the result, because pressure extraction can't be done by hand, so the hardware has a reason to exist. It also built a direct relationship with customers around the consumable. **Matches: business model, market. Differs: mechanism, since its hardware can't be bypassed.** *Source: well-established knowledge, not the library.*

Not cited because they match too few dimensions: MoviePass, Webvan, Instacart, AMC A-List. Quibi only partly matches: it lost to a free alternative (here, your customers' hands), but its market and model are different.

## Independent opinions

**A · Devil's advocate**: **Against shipping.** This is Juicero's exact cause of death, found by your own users before a journalist found it. The most fragile assumption is that customers pay €600 for a process rather than a result. If hands give "almost the same juice", the machine's value is close to zero and the QR check looks like a lock, not a feature. Juicero cut its price by about 40% within months and still died. What would have to be true for me to be wrong: blind tests show a clear, repeatable difference in yield, taste or hygiene that customers value at hundreds of euros. Confidence: **high**.

**B · First principles**: **Depends**, and the question is wrong. Your actual product is fresh juice in a sealed pack, and the press is one way to deliver it. Third option: launch the packs as the business (subscription, retail, offices) and either drop the machine or turn it into a cheap accessory. Alternatively, redesign the pack so the machine does something hands really can't, such as cold-pressing whole produce or mixing on demand. What would have to be true: the packs sell repeatedly on their own. Juicero's lesson points this way: "the value lived in the consumable". Confidence: **medium-high**.

**C · Expansionist**: **Depends.** There is real upside in a kitchen device with recurring revenue (Nespresso), Wi-Fi data on consumption, and freshness verification as a trust signal for B2B buyers like offices, hotels and gyms, where hygiene and traceability count more than convenience. You aren't Juicero *if* you sell to buyers who value auditability over squeezing. What would have to be true: some segment pays for verification and hygiene, not for pressure. **(opinion)**: I have no data on that segment. Confidence: **low-medium**.

**D · Outsider**: **Against shipping as is.** As a new customer, the first thing I'd ask is why I need a €600 box to squeeze a bag. If a friend shows me it works by hand, I feel scammed, and the QR code makes it worse ("they're checking up on me"). The obvious version for me is to sell the juice and maybe give a squeezer for free. **(opinion)**, but it matches what happened to Juicero in public. Confidence: **high**.

**E · Executor**: **Don't ship yet.** Tomorrow at 9:00, put the hand-versus-machine comparison in writing: yield in ml per pack, a blind taste test and time. It takes two days and costs a few packs. If you commit to a production run, a fix later means re-tooling the pack or the machine, which is slow and expensive. What blocks this is an investor narrative built on the hardware, and someone has to tell them. Confidence: **high**.

**F · Customer (specialist: decides whether anyone pays)**: **Against at €600.** Juicero's buyers were early adopters, and it still had to cut to $400 within months. Your testers *already found* the workaround without being told, so customers will too, and returns plus public reviews will follow. What would have to be true: testers prefer the machine in a blind test *and* say they would pay for it. Confidence: **medium-high**.

**G · Investor/partner (specialist: investors "love the hardware")**: **Against shipping unchanged.** Investors loving the hardware is a risk, not a validation. Juicero had top-tier VCs and that didn't help. Telling them now, with a plan, keeps their trust, while them reading it in the press destroys it. A "packs-first" business can still raise money if retention is strong. Confidence: **medium**.

## Blind cross-review

- **Strongest**: A, because it rests on a near-exact precedent plus your own test data. E is close behind for turning that into a cheap test.
- **Weakest**: C. The B2B verification segment is speculation with no data, and it rationalises keeping the hardware.
- **Flaw found**: B assumes the packs sell on their own, which nobody has tested yet. D assumes every customer will find the hand squeeze, which is likely but not proven, although the testers' behaviour supports it.

## Debate round

**C (expansionist)**: "Juicero targeted homes. If our buyers are offices or hotels, where hygiene and verified freshness matter, the machine is a compliance tool, not a squeezer."
**A (devil's advocate)**: "Then that's a different product with different buyers, and you haven't tested it. The consumer launch you're about to ship is the Juicero case. Test the B2B idea separately and don't use it as cover for shipping."

Chair: A wins on the evidence. C's idea stays as something to test, not a reason to ship.

## Pre-mortem (12 months on, it failed)

1. **The "you don't need it" moment goes public.** A review, video or journalist shows the hand squeeze. *Early signal*: testers already did it on their own, which is what happened to Juicero via Bloomberg in April 2017.
2. **Price collapse.** €600 doesn't convert, you discount, the margin goes, and the subsidised hardware burns cash. *Early signal*: low pre-order conversion, or a discount discussed within the first few months. Juicero went from $699 to $400.
3. **The QR code reads as DRM.** Users resent being blocked or tracked, and workarounds spread. *Early signal*: complaints about blocked packs or Wi-Fi needs in support tickets or reviews, as in the Keurig 2.0 backlash.

## Chair's verdict

**Decision**: Ship the €600 Wi-Fi press now, or fix the product and offer first.
**Verdict**: **Don't ship as is. First find out** whether the machine does anything customers would pay for that hands can't do. · Confidence: **8/10**

**Agreement**: Every seat except the expansionist (C) treats the hand-squeeze finding as a likely deal-breaker for a €600 consumer device. It is the same flaw that killed Juicero, and you found it *before* launch, which is a real advantage.
**Real disagreement**: whether there is a segment (B2B, hygiene and traceability) where the machine plus QR verification has value of its own. That idea is untested and plausible, but it is a different product.
**Assumption that matters most**: "the machine adds meaningful value over hands." → *Cheapest test*: a blind comparison with 20–30 people. Measure yield (ml) and taste preference, and ask what they would pay for each option.
**Closest precedent**: **Juicero**. You are Juicero on market, model and flaw. The only difference is that you know now. Your way out is Nespresso's: make the machine physically necessary to the result, or make the pack the business.

**Next step (48 h)**: run the hand-versus-machine test on yield, blind taste and time, write up the result in one page, and share it with investors along with two options. Option 1: a packs-first business with a cheap or free press. Option 2: redesign the pack or machine so hands can't get the same result.
**Kill criterion** (thresholds are proposals): if the machine extracts less than ~15% more juice than hands, *or* blind tasters can't tell the difference better than chance, then **don't ship the €600 device**. If the packs alone don't reach a repeat purchase rate you consider viable within 8 weeks of a small pilot, rethink the whole offer, not just the hardware.
