**Frame.** You need to decide whether to put most of your large content budget into a premium launch slate, or to stage it behind proof that people will pay. The app costs €5/month and offers 7-minute episodes by famous directors, made for watching on a phone while commuting. The competition is free short video.

- **Reversibility**: mostly a one-way door. Once you commission directors' work the money is spent. Pricing and positioning can still change later.
- **Stakes**: high money, high reputation (public launch with famous names), time.
- **Horizon**: before you sign the content commitments.

**Assumptions I'm making, since there are no numbers yet:** no paying users or pilot data exist. "Large budget" means most of the company's capital. The plan is a standalone subscription app, not a bundle with an existing service. The plan is to launch with a full slate.

## Precedents

```
☠ Quibi (2018–2020): died of no-market-need, competition and timing.
   Matches: market (premium short video), business model (paid b2c subscription),
   failure mechanism (paying for content before checking that people will pay instead of watching free video).
   Differs: timing. Quibi launched in April 2020, when nobody was commuting. You would launch into normal commuting.

☠ MoviePass (2017–2019): died of pricing and unit economics.
   Matches: market (entertainment subscription), business model (b2c subscription).
   Differs: mechanism. Its problem was a price below the cost of each film, not missing demand. It is relevant only
   to your €5 price: check that your price works at the usage levels you actually see.

✓ AMC Stubs A-List: survived the subscription pricing trap that sank MoviePass.
   It set the price from its own data on how often members go, and it controlled the cost of what it sold.
   Matches: market, business model. Differs: mechanism (it is a pricing survivor, not a demand survivor).

✓ Netflix originals (from general knowledge, not in the library): it started commissioning big-budget
   original series (House of Cards, 2013) only after years of streaming had built a subscriber base and data
   on what people watched. The content spending came after the audience and the habit existed.
   Matches: market, business model. Differs: format (long-form, TV).

✓ Paid vertical micro-drama apps such as ReelShort (from general knowledge, not verified in this run): they make
   money from short, phone-first episodes by producing cheaply, testing many titles, and charging per episode or
   by subscription after cliffhangers. Matches: market, format, mechanism. Differs: no famous names, low cost per episode.
   This is evidence that people will pay for short video on a phone. It is not evidence that they will pay for
   *prestige* short video.
```

Considered and rejected:
- **Webvan.** It died of premature scaling, but it matches only on mechanism, so it is below the citation threshold.
- **Juicero.** It matches on none of the four dimensions.

No precedent in the library shows a premium-director short-video service surviving the trap Quibi fell into. Quibi's own shutdown letter named two possible causes: the idea wasn't strong enough to justify a standalone service, or the timing. You remove the timing problem. **You do not remove the first cause.**

## Independent opinions

**1. Devil's advocate: Against · Confidence: high**
This is Quibi without the pandemic, and Quibi's founders did not blame only the timing. They also said the idea itself might not justify a standalone service. Quibi's warning signs were that the commuting use was never tested with paying users and that few people converted from the free trial to paid. You have exactly that untested assumption. Famous directors make good press, not a habit. Quibi had big names and $1.75B and still could not buy the habit.
*Would have to be true for me to be wrong:* commuters pay €5 for this when TikTok, YouTube and Reels are free on the same phone, on the same train.

**2. First principles: Depends · Confidence: medium**
What problem does this solve? "Fill 7 minutes on the train" is already solved for free. "See new work by directors I admire" is a different job, and it may not need an app or a subscription. Third option: test the core idea before building a platform. Commission 3–5 episodes, sell them as one paid anthology or pre-sale, or license them to an existing platform, and measure who pays (opinion). The content spending comes after demand shows up, as Netflix did with its originals.
*Would have to be true:* the directors' work is worth paying for in its own right, whatever the commute.

**3. Expansionist: For, with conditions · Confidence: medium**
You are not Quibi on timing: you launch into real commuting. Paid vertical short-drama apps show that people will pay for short episodes on a phone. So the format is not dead. Quibi's mistakes were its execution and its timing (opinion, partly backed by the micro-drama evidence). Famous directors could give you real press and a back catalogue, and the episodes could be licensed to bigger platforms later.
*Would have to be true:* the micro-drama buyers' reason to pay (cliffhangers, cheap per episode) carries over to prestige content. They succeed with the opposite recipe.

**4. Outsider: Against · Confidence: medium**
I'm on the metro. I already have Netflix, maybe Spotify, and TikTok for free. Why would I add a fourth €5 subscription for 7-minute films? I don't know which directors these are, and I'm not sure I would recognise them. If my connection drops in the tunnel, does it still work? "Premium" doesn't tell me what I get that I can't get free (opinion).
*Would have to be true:* the offer is obvious within 5 seconds and works offline underground.

**5. Executor: Depends · Confidence: medium**
Tomorrow at 9:00, either you sign contracts with directors (the money is gone in months, and the slate is fixed before you have any user data) or you build a pilot. A full launch slate means long production times and no learning loop. A pilot means a few episodes, a paywall, a few thousand real commuters, and conversion data in 6–8 weeks. What blocks the pilot: directors may not want to make "test" content, and an option or first-look deal might fix that (opinion).
*Would have to be true:* you can buy a small first batch of content and options on the rest.

**6. Finance (chosen because the question is about where to put the budget): Against an all-in bet · Confidence: high**
At €5/month, every €1M of content needs about 200,000 paid subscriber-months just to pay back the content. That is before app store fees, marketing and ongoing production. *Estimate, gross figures.* Short episodes are used up quickly, so subscribers need a steady supply. You will need a budget for ongoing content after launch, which a launch-heavy plan uses up. AMC's lesson is to price from real usage data. You have none yet.
*Would have to be true:* trial-to-paid conversion and month-3 retention good enough that each subscriber's lifetime value covers the cost of new content.

**7. Customer (chosen because demand is the key unknown): Depends · Confidence: medium**
There are probably two different buyers: film fans who come for the directors, and commuters who want to pass the time. Film fans may pay, but they are a niche. Commuters are a mass market, but they are already served for free. Building for both blurs the offer. Pick one and test it (opinion).
*Would have to be true:* one of the two groups shows that it will pay in a real test, not in a survey.

## Pre-mortem

It is 12 months later and the big launch failed. The three most likely reasons:

1. **Free trial users didn't convert.** The launch press brought downloads, but few people paid €5 once the trial ended.
   *Early signal:* trial-to-paid conversion in the first 30 days. This is the same signal that appeared at Quibi.
2. **The commuting habit never formed.** People watched the famous names once and did not come back daily or weekly.
   *Early signal:* week-4 retention and sessions per week falling after launch week.
3. **The content ran out before the budget for new content did.** The slate was finished in a few weeks of commutes, and there was no money left to keep releasing episodes.
   *Early signal:* minutes watched per subscriber against total catalogue hours. MoviePass is a partial parallel: costs grow with use.

## Chair's verdict

**Decision**: whether to put the large content budget into a premium launch slate for a €5/month short-video app for commuters.

**Verdict**: **Don't bet the budget on launch content. First find out whether commuters will pay €5 for prestige short video instead of watching free video.** Spend a small share of the budget on a paid pilot, and release the rest in tranches tied to conversion and retention. · Confidence: 7/10

**Agreement**: Every seat agrees the critical unknown is willingness to pay against the free alternative. None of you have that data yet. Famous names alone do not create a habit.

**Real disagreement**: whether Quibi failed because of the idea or because of the timing. Its own founders couldn't say which. That uncertainty is the reason to test before spending, not a reason to assume timing was the problem.

**Assumption that matters most**: "Commuters will pay €5/month for premium 7-minute episodes when free short video is on the same phone."
→ **Cheapest test**: commission or license 3–5 episodes and launch a closed paid pilot in one city or market with the real €5 paywall, after a short trial. Measure trial-to-paid conversion and week-4 retention. Run a cheaper variant (not famous directors) in parallel to see whether the names actually move conversion.

**Closest precedent**: **Quibi**. You match it on market, business model and the untested assumption, and you are only different on timing. The pattern to copy is Netflix's: spend heavily on content only after the audience and the data exist.

**Next step (48 h)**: write a one-page pilot specification. It should cover the market, the 3–5 titles, the €5 paywall, the trial length, and the target and minimum acceptable trial-to-paid conversion and week-4 retention. Send it to two or three directors as a paid pilot with an option on more episodes, instead of a full slate contract.

**Kill criterion**: if the pilot's trial-to-paid conversion or week-4 retention falls below the minimums you wrote in the spec *before seeing the data*, do not release the main content budget. Instead, change the model: bundle or license the episodes, sell per episode, or target film fans. Set a date, for example 10 weeks after the pilot launches. *The thresholds are yours to set. Set them in advance so you can't move the goalposts later.*
