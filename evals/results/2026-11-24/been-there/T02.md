## Step 0 · Frame

**Decision**: Decide whether to launch an unlimited cinema subscription at €9.99/month now to grab the market, or to change the price, cap or cost structure first. We pay cinemas about €9 per visit, and the plan counts on light users to cover the rest.

- **Reversibility**: close to a **one-way door**. Losses grow with every subscriber you sign. Raising the price or adding caps after launch is possible, but it is the exact sequence that destroyed trust in MoviePass (see below).
- **Stakes**: money (cash burn rises with adoption), reputation (emergency rule changes) and partners (cinema contracts).
- **Horizon**: not stated. I'm assuming no hard deadline.

**Assumptions I'm using, since I'm not asking questions:** you don't own cinemas. €9 is the full retail ticket you pay per visit, with no wholesale discount and no share of concessions. "Unlimited" means no cap on visits. You're in Spain or another euro market. VAT and payment fees come out of the €9.99, so you keep less than that per subscriber. I don't know the exact net figure, so treat it as an estimate.

**The basic numbers** (simple arithmetic, not a forecast): each visit costs about €9, so the plan loses money once a subscriber's average goes above **about 1.1 visits a month**. That is before VAT, fees, acquisition cost and support. Net of those, break-even is **below one visit a month**.

## Step 1 · Precedents

```
Precedents
☠ MoviePass (2017–2019): died of pricing and unit economics. In Aug 2017 it cut
  the price to $9.95/month for one film a day while paying cinemas the full
  ticket price, so it lost money as soon as a subscriber went twice a month.
  Frequent moviegoers signed up first, and chains refused to share concessions.
  Matches: market, business model, failure mechanism (almost the same price and
  cost structure). Differs: region (US vs EU) and timing (2017 vs 2026).
✓ AMC Stubs A-List (2018–): faced the same trap and survived. It owns the cinemas
  (so it was "essentially paying itself"), kept concession revenue, charged
  about twice MoviePass's price ($19.95), capped use at 3 films a week, and set
  the price from its own data on how often people go. It estimated subscribers
  had to average about 2.5–3 visits a month for the plan to stay profitable.
  Matches: market, model, mechanism. Differs: AMC controls its cost per ticket
  and you don't.
```

Considered and not cited (they match fewer than 2 dimensions):
- **Webvan**: only the "scale before the economics work" mechanism matches.
- **Quibi**: the market and subscription model are close, but it died of weak demand, not of paying full price for usage.

Survivor evidence beyond the library, based on general knowledge and not checked in this run: the unlimited cinema plans that have lasted in Europe (UGC Illimité in France, Cineworld Unlimited in the UK) are also run by the chains that own the screens. That fits the AMC pattern.

Pattern: in this library, every survivor controls its cost per visit. The one company that paid full retail price per visit and relied on low usage died.

## Step 2 · Independent opinions

Specialist seats:
- **Finance**: the whole question rests on unit economics.
- **Cinema partner**: the only way out of the trap is the price you pay cinemas, and they hold that lever.

**1. Devil's advocate: Against · high confidence**
The fragile assumption is "gym-style low usage". At a gym, an extra visit costs the gym almost nothing. Here every visit costs you about €9, so heavy users don't share a fixed cost, they each add a new bill. Worse, the selection works against you. People who go 3–4 times a month see the biggest saving and sign up first, while people who go 0–1 times have little reason to join. That is exactly what happened to MoviePass: its frequent moviegoers joined first, and its parent said monthly losses would grow as the service became more popular. For the plan to work, most subscribers would have to pay every month and go less than once. People who rarely go tend to cancel (opinion).

**2. First principles: Depends (on cost per visit) · high confidence**
The real problem being solved is not "cheap films for consumers". It is that cinemas have empty seats on weekdays and off-peak sessions, and an empty seat earns them nothing. The only lasting business here buys that unused capacity below retail price: wholesale off-peak seats, a revenue share, or a cut of concessions. A third option is a **capped, off-peak pass** priced on an agreed wholesale rate. The other version is a white-label subscription that you run for a cinema chain, which carries the cost, the way AMC does for itself. What would have to be true: at least one chain agrees to a rate per visit well below €9.

**3. Expansionist: Depends · medium confidence**
The upside is real. AMC A-List shows the demand exists, and a subscription that drives traffic on weekdays and sells popcorn is valuable to cinemas. Why this case doesn't have to be MoviePass: you're starting in 2026 with MoviePass as a public warning, cinemas know the model, and you can negotiate before launch instead of after. But "grab the market" at a loss only works if you can later lower the cost per visit or raise the price without a backlash. MoviePass tried exactly that and failed. So I support the market, not this price.

**4. Outsider: Against this version · medium confidence**
As a customer, "unlimited for €9.99 when one ticket costs €9" sounds too good to last. My first thought is "how long until they change the rules?" If I go twice a month, I sign up today. If I go once every two months, I don't bother. The people you need, the ones who pay and don't come, are the people least interested in the offer (opinion).

**5. Executor: Against launching now · medium confidence**
Tomorrow at 9:00 you need signed cinema agreements, a ticketing integration (codes, apps or a payment card), and cash to cover the gap between charging €9.99 and paying €9 per visit. The blocker isn't technology. It's that each new active user increases your cash need. Before you scale, you would need a cash runway model with usage scenarios, plus at least one partner at a below-retail rate. Neither exists yet (going on what you've told me).

**6. Finance: Against · high confidence**
Illustrative mix (an estimate, not data): 40% of subscribers go 0 times a month, 30% go twice, 30% go 4 times. That averages 1.8 visits, about €16 of cost against under €9.99 of net revenue. You lose about €6+ per subscriber per month, before acquisition costs. Even an optimistic average of 1 visit leaves almost nothing once VAT and fees are paid. AMC, which owned the cinemas, still needed $19.95 and a cap. What would have to be true: average visits below about 0.8 a month after selection. No precedent here shows that.

**7. Cinema partner: Depends · medium confidence**
From the cinema's side, you're a customer paying full price, which is good for them, until you ask for a discount. Chains refused to share concessions with MoviePass. Your leverage is filling off-peak seats. Your weakness is that a chain can launch its own pass, as AMC did when MoviePass grew. You can offer chains and independents a weekday or off-peak volume deal. You can't build on the assumption that they'll subsidise you later.

## Step 5 · Pre-mortem

"It's 12 months later and the €9.99 launch failed. Why?"

1. **Heavy users joined first, and losses grew with growth.**
   Early signal: the average number of visits per active subscriber in the first cohort is above about 1 a month. Precedent: MoviePass's parent warned that losses would rise as the service became more popular.
2. **Emergency rule changes destroyed trust.**
   Early signal: you're already modelling price rises, blackout dates or caps within the first quarter. Precedent: MoviePass in 2018 went to $14.95, blocked new releases, then cut to 3 films a month.
3. **Cinemas wouldn't share upside, or launched their own pass.**
   Early signal: no chain agrees to a below-retail rate or a concessions share, or a chain announces its own subscription. Precedents: chains refused to share concessions with MoviePass, and AMC launched A-List as a rival.

## Step 6 · Chair's verdict

**Decision**: Whether to launch unlimited cinema access at €9.99/month while paying about €9 per visit, relying on light users.

**Verdict**: **Don't, not at this price and cost structure.** Instead, launch only once you control the cost per visit: a below-retail wholesale or off-peak rate, a usage cap, and a price set from real visit data. · Confidence: 9/10

**Agreement**: Every seat agrees that the gym analogy breaks because each visit costs you about €9, and that the people most attracted to the offer are the ones who cost you most. This is the MoviePass structure almost exactly: $9.95/month, full ticket price, frequent users signing up first.

**Real disagreement**: Only about the alternative. B and G favour a business built on wholesale off-peak seats or white-labelling for chains. C favours staying consumer-facing with caps. Both are possible. Neither is possible at €9.99 unlimited with full-price tickets.

**Assumption that matters most**: That subscribers will average under about 1 visit a month *after* self-selection. → Cheapest test: a pre-sale or waitlist landing page with the real price, where people pick a plan and state their current visits per month. Look at who wants it, not at how many sign up.

**Closest precedent**: **MoviePass.** You are them on price, cost per visit and the reliance on low usage. You are *not* AMC, because you don't own the screens, don't keep concessions and have no usage data. To get closer to AMC, you have to borrow its three levers: control of cost, a cap, and pricing from data.

**Next step (48 h)**:
1. Build a one-page spreadsheet of usage scenarios, from 0.5 to 3 visits a month, giving break-even visits net of VAT and fees.
2. Email 3 cinemas (one chain, two independents) asking for an off-peak wholesale rate per seat and whether they'd share concessions.

The output is verifiable: a break-even table plus 3 sent requests.

**Kill criterion**: if no cinema has agreed in writing to a rate per visit low enough that your break-even is **at least 2 visits a month** by **31 January 2027**, don't launch an unlimited plan. Either switch to a capped discount pass or pitch a white-label pass to a chain. If you do launch a pilot, stop scaling as soon as the first cohort averages above break-even visits for 2 months in a row.
