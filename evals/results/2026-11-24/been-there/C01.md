**Decision**: Decide whether to keep building a peer-to-peer marketplace for short stays in hosts' homes, starting with events where hotels sell out, or to stop, given that you already have a few paying hosts and guests and that most people tell you "nobody will sleep in a stranger's home".

- **Reversibility**: mostly a two-way door. Continuing a small, event-focused pilot is cheap to stop. Raising money, hiring, or launching in many cities would be the one-way part.
- **Stakes**: time and money; reputation and safety (a single bad incident in someone's home); regulatory exposure (short-term rental and tax rules, which vary by city).
- **Horizon**: the next event where hotels sell out.

**Assumptions** (I'm not asking questions, so here's what I'm going with): "a few" means fewer than about 20 completed stays. Hosts and guests are paying through your site, not as favours to friends. You haven't raised significant money yet. You're starting now (2026), so Airbnb and Booking.com already exist in your market. If this were 2008, the analysis below would be the same except for the competition point.

### Precedents

**Note:** this idea is close to a description of Airbnb's origin. AirBed & Breakfast started in 2007–2008 by renting air mattresses in the founders' San Francisco flat during a design conference when hotels were full, then targeted sold-out events such as the 2008 Democratic National Convention in Denver. None of the 6 entries in the precedent library are short-term rental companies. The cases below come from well-established public history, not from the library, and I give no figures where I'm not sure of them.

```
✓ Airbnb (2008–) survived "strangers won't do this" by starting where the alternative was
  nothing (sold-out events), then building trust tools: real photos, two-way reviews, profiles,
  payment held by the platform, and, after a 2011 incident in which a host's home was
  trashed, a host guarantee and a 24/7 support line.
  Matches: market, model, mechanism (trust). Differs: timing. You face an incumbent; they didn't.
✓ Couchsurfing (2004–) showed years earlier that large numbers of people will sleep in a
  stranger's home, for free, when there are reference systems and community norms.
  Matches: market, mechanism (trust). Differs: model (free, not paid).
✓ Instacart (library) avoided Webvan's death by using existing infrastructure instead of building
  its own. Your "inventory" is spare rooms that already exist, so it's the same asset-light logic.
  Matches: model (asset-light marketplace), mechanism (premature-scaling risk). Differs: market.
☠ Webvan (library) died of building for many cities before one market worked.
  Matches: mechanism (premature-scaling risk). Differs: market, model. Cited only as a warning
  about expanding too early.
```

**No strong failure precedent found** for "trust in home-sharing killed the company". I won't force one. The failure risks that actually apply are listed in the pre-mortem: regulation, a safety incident, competition, and demand that only exists during events.

### Independent opinions

Specialist seats: **Risk & compliance**, because hosting in a home carries both a physical safety risk and legal risk from city rules. **Customer (host side)**, because supply is the hard side of this marketplace.

**A · Devil's advocate**: *Against* (as a standalone business in 2026)
Strongest argument: the "strangers" objection has already been answered, by Airbnb. That's the problem. Your real risks are now competition and regulation, not trust. A guest who needs a bed at a sold-out event will open Airbnb first, and hosts already list there. Event demand also spikes for a few days a year, so you'd have to win the same customers again at every event. *(opinion, based on Airbnb's position)*
For this to be wrong: you can show a segment Airbnb serves badly, such as very short notice, a cheap room in a shared home, or a local event it doesn't cover.
Confidence: medium.

**B · First principles**: *Depends*
The real problem isn't "rooms in homes". It's that a fixed number of visitors arrive at one place and time, and there aren't enough beds. That suggests a third option: sell to the event organisers, not to travellers. Become the official overflow housing for festivals, conferences and sports events. You'd acquire customers once per organiser instead of once per guest, and the organiser lends you its credibility, which helps with trust.
For this to be right: organisers care about housing shortages enough to promote or pay for a solution.
Confidence: medium.

**C · Expansionist**: *For*
You aren't asking "will strangers do this?" in the abstract. You already have paying hosts and guests, and that beats what "everyone says". Airbnb is the counterexample to the skeptics: same objection, same entry point, and it worked. Sold-out events are an excellent wedge because guests have no alternative, so they'll accept an air mattress, and hosts earn money in a weekend.
For this to be right: people who've tried it once book or host again, and recommend it.
Confidence: medium-high.

**D · Outsider**: *Depends*
If I'm a guest, my first question is: is this person safe, and is the bed real? If I'm a host: who pays if something gets broken or stolen? If your site doesn't answer both in the first 10 seconds, I leave. I'd also ask: why not just use Airbnb? *(opinion)*
For this to be right: you can answer "why you and not Airbnb" in one sentence.
Confidence: medium.

**E · Executor**: *For, kept small*
Tomorrow: call every host and guest you've had and ask whether they'd do it again, what almost stopped them, and whether they'd have used Airbnb instead. Then pick one upcoming sold-out event and try to fill every room you can around it. Blockers: few hosts in that city, and no answers yet on payment protection and damage policy. Don't build features; do the work by hand.
For this to be right: you can sign up hosts by hand at a reasonable rate.
Confidence: high.

**F · Risk & compliance**: *Depends*
One incident in someone's home can end a small brand. Airbnb, with far more resources, had to bring in a guarantee and 24/7 support after the 2011 case. Many cities restrict short-term rentals: New York's 2023 registration law, and Barcelona's plan to phase out tourist-apartment licences, are well-known examples. A room in the host's home while the host is present is often treated more leniently than a whole flat, but this has to be checked city by city. You also need ID checks, clear terms, and damage cover (insurance or a deposit).
Confidence: high.

**G · Customer (host side)**: *For, if hosts repeat*
A few paying hosts is your most valuable data point. The question is whether they'd host at the next event without you chasing them. Hosts mainly fear damage, guests who won't leave, and awkwardness. Make one-off, few-night stays at known dates easy and capped, and that fear shrinks. *(opinion)*
Confidence: medium.

### Blind cross-review

- **Strongest**: B, because it changes who the customer is and solves both the cost of acquiring customers and the trust problem in one move. Also F, because it's the only opinion naming a risk that could end the company in a single night.
- **Weakest**: C. It leans on Airbnb's existence as proof for you. Airbnb proves the behaviour exists, not that a second platform can win it.
- **Flaw in A**: it assumes guests always check Airbnb first. Sold-out events are exactly when Airbnb listings run out or prices jump. That's untested either way, and your existing guests can tell you.
- **Flaw in E**: "fill one event by hand" doesn't show whether there's demand between events.

### Debate (A vs C: real disagreement, medium confidence on both sides)

- **A**: "Your few customers prove the behaviour exists. Airbnb proved that already. They don't prove people would choose you over Airbnb. Without that, you've rebuilt a feature of an incumbent."
- **C**: "Then measure exactly that. Ask every guest whether they looked at Airbnb first. If an event really is sold out, supply on every platform is short, so the platform with extra local hosts wins those nights. That's a niche Airbnb doesn't target."

The chair's view: C wins on whether to *continue*, and A wins on what to *test*.

### Pre-mortem: it's November 2027 and this failed. Why?

1. **No reason to choose you over Airbnb.** Guests found the same hosts on Airbnb, or hosts left for more reach. *Early signal:* your guests say they "checked Airbnb first", or your hosts also list there. (Precedent: none in the library. This is the competition risk.)
2. **Demand only during events, with no repeat use.** Every event meant starting from zero, and the cost of acquiring customers ate your margin. *Early signal:* fewer than half of your hosts return for the second event without being asked again.
3. **A safety or legal incident.** One bad stay, or a city fine against your hosts, made the press. *Early signal:* hosts ask "am I allowed to do this?" or "who covers damage?", and you don't have a written answer. (Airbnb's 2011 incident is the warning sign here.)

**Decision**: Keep building a home-stay marketplace that starts with sold-out events.
**Verdict**: Do it like this: keep going, but stay tightly focused on events, add trust and safety basics before anything else, and test the event-organiser channel. Don't raise money or expand to new cities until hosts and guests repeat. · Confidence: 7/10

**Agreement**: The "strangers won't do it" objection is the weakest argument against you. Paying customers and Airbnb's history both contradict it. Trust tools and legal checks are not optional.
**Real disagreement**: whether there's room for a new platform next to Airbnb (A vs C). This can be resolved with data you can collect this month.
**Assumption that matters most**: people choose *you* for sold-out events, not just home-sharing in general → **cheapest test**: ask every past guest and host 3 questions (Did you check Airbnb or Booking first? Would you use us again for the next event? What almost stopped you?). Then fill one upcoming event by hand and count how many bookings come from people who couldn't find a bed elsewhere.
**Closest precedent**: Airbnb. You match its starting point almost exactly. The difference is that it had no incumbent to beat, and you do. So your advantage has to come from something Airbnb doesn't do: dedicated supply for specific events, or partnerships with organisers.

**Next step (48 h)**: Call all your past hosts and guests and ask the 3 questions above. Write a one-page host policy (ID checks, damage cover or deposit, house rules, emergency contact). Email 3 organisers of upcoming sold-out events offering to be their overflow housing.
**Kill criterion**: if by **28 February 2027** fewer than 40% of your existing hosts agree to host again, or more than half of your guests say they'd have booked through Airbnb or Booking anyway, stop working on a standalone marketplace. Either switch to the organiser-partnership model (B) or stop. The thresholds are my estimates; adjust them to your numbers.
