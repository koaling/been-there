## Step 0 · Frame

**Decision**: Whether to launch an online pet store that sells heavy, low-margin staples (big bags of dog food, cat litter) with free shipping, priced below supermarkets, and backed by a large TV campaign, given that shipping a 15 kg bag already costs more than the margin on it.

- **Reversibility**: Mixed. Prices and shipping policy can be changed later (two-way door). The TV campaign can't: once that money is spent it is gone, and a customer base you acquired on "cheaper than the supermarket + free shipping" is hard to re-price later.
- **Stakes**: Money (a negative margin on every order, plus the ad spend), and your reputation with the customers you acquire.
- **Horizon**: Before you commit the TV budget.

**Assumptions** (no clarifying questions in this run): the shipping cost per 15 kg bag is higher than the gross margin *per order*, not just per bag. Most orders are one or two heavy items. You use third-party parcel carriers, not your own fleet. No numbers were given for basket size, repeat rate or ad budget.

**The short answer to "volume will fix it, right?"**: No, not as described. Volume fixes *fixed* costs. Your problem is a *variable* cost (weight-based parcel shipping) that is higher than your margin on every unit. More orders multiply that loss. They don't dilute it.

## Step 2 · Independent opinions

Specialist seats: **Finance** (the question is basically a contribution-margin question) and **Customer** (whether price-led customers stay).

**1. Devil's advocate**: **Against.**
Strongest argument: this is the Pets.com setup almost exactly: heavy staples, below-cost price, free shipping, mass TV. Like MoviePass, every new customer makes the loss bigger. The most fragile assumption is "volume lowers shipping cost enough." Carrier volume discounts are real, but they shave a percentage off a weight-driven price. They don't usually flip a negative per-order margin to positive. The TV campaign speeds up the bleeding.
Has to be true for me to be wrong: carrier quotes at your realistic year-1 volume push shipping per 15 kg order below your gross margin.
Confidence: **high**.

**2. First principles**: **Against the plan as stated; for a different design.**
The real problem isn't "sell pet food online." It's "be the place a pet owner never has to think about again." Customers want two things: never running out, and not carrying 15 kg home. Price is only third. A third option: recurring delivery (subscription) at supermarket parity, not below it. Make baskets bigger so shipping is spread over more items (treats, litter plus food). Offer free shipping only above a threshold. Offer pickup points for single heavy bags. Shipping a 15 kg bag on its own is the worst unit you can sell.
Has to be true: customers will accept parity pricing in exchange for convenience and automatic re-ordering.
Confidence: **medium-high** (Chewy precedent).

**3. Expansionist**: **Depends.**
The upside is real: pet food is bought again and again, in predictable amounts, so lifetime value can be high. Chewy proves the category can be won online. Why this needn't be Pets.com: today's carriers, pickup networks and subscription tools didn't exist in 2000, and a customer who reorders every month for years can pay back a first-order loss. But that only works if the *repeat* orders make money. A price that loses money on every order, forever, gets no help from lifetime value.
Has to be true: repeat orders have positive contribution, and repeat rates are high.
Confidence: **medium**.

**4. Outsider**: **Against (opinion).**
As a customer: "Cheaper than the supermarket, delivered free? Great, I'll buy only the dog food bag and nothing else." That's the order you lose the most on. I'd also wonder how long it lasts. When prices go up, I'm gone, because I came for the price, not for you. Nothing in the pitch says why I'd stay.
Has to be true: there's a reason to stay other than price.
Confidence: **medium**.

**5. Executor**: **Against launching TV; for a small pilot.**
Tomorrow at 9:00 I need one spreadsheet: per order, price − cost of goods − pick/pack − packaging − shipping − payment fees − damage/returns. Do it for three typical baskets. Then get quotes from 2–3 carriers or 3PLs for 15 kg parcels at today's volume and at 10× volume. TV can't be tested in small steps. Search and social ads can, so a 1–2 region pilot comes first. What would block it: no carrier will commit to volume pricing without real volume. Use estimates and mark them as such.
Confidence: **high**.

**6. Finance**: **Against.**
Volume spreads fixed costs: the website, the team, the warehouse lease, and the TV spend itself. It doesn't touch variable cost per order. If the contribution per order is negative, the more you sell, the more cash you burn. The TV campaign adds a big fixed cost that can only be recovered from *positive* contribution, and you have none. So the campaign can never pay for itself under this pricing. That's arithmetic, not opinion.
Has to be true: contribution per order turns positive at a reachable volume.
Confidence: **high**.

**7. Customer**: **Depends.**
Pet owners are loyal to a food brand and a routine, not necessarily to a store. A price-led promise attracts the most price-sensitive shoppers, and they switch the fastest (the MoviePass dynamic: the costliest users sign up first). Convenience features (automatic reorder, reminders, reliable delivery dates) build stickiness. A discount alone doesn't.
Has to be true: you can win customers on convenience, not just price.
Confidence: **medium**.

## Step 4 · Debate round

Real disagreement: **C (Expansionist, depends/medium)** vs **F (Finance, against/high)**.

**Expansionist**: "Every subscription business loses money on the first order. Chewy lost money for years and won. If you judge on first-order margin, you'd never build a recurring-revenue business."

**Finance**: "Losing on the *first* order to acquire a customer is fine. Losing on *every* order is not. The user said shipping a 15 kg bag costs more than the margin, and that's true on order 1 and on order 40. Chewy's path ran through minimum baskets, subscriptions and a richer mix, not through free shipping on everything below supermarket prices. Show me positive contribution on repeat orders and I'll back the TV spend."

Chair: Finance carries the round. The Expansionist's point holds only after the pricing and shipping design is fixed.

## Step 5 · Pre-mortem

It's November 2027 and the plan failed. The three most likely causes:

1. **Every order lost money, and the TV campaign multiplied the orders.**
   Early warning: contribution per order stays negative while revenue grows; carrier invoices grow faster than gross margin.
   Precedent: MoviePass (losses rose with popularity), Pets.com.
2. **Customers bought only the heaviest, cheapest item.** Your average basket ended up as one bag of food or litter.
   Early warning: average items per order under ~1.5 and a high share of single-SKU heavy orders. That threshold is an estimate.
   Precedent: Pets.com (heavy, cheap items were the worst orders to ship).
3. **The price-led customers left when you raised prices or added shipping fees.**
   Early warning: low 60/90-day repeat rate, and churn spiking after any price change.
   Precedent: MoviePass's repeated emergency changes in 2018.

## Step 6 · Chair's verdict

**Decision**: Launch an online pet store selling heavy staples with free shipping, below supermarket prices, backed by a large TV campaign.
**Verdict**: **Don't, as designed. Do it like this:** fix contribution per order first, pilot without TV, and only scale spend once repeat orders are profitable. · Confidence: **8/10**

**Agreement**: Every seat agrees that volume doesn't fix a variable cost that's higher than the margin. More orders mean more loss. The TV campaign shouldn't come before positive per-order economics.

**Real disagreement**: Whether a first-order loss can be justified by lifetime value. Yes, but only if repeat orders are profitable. Today they wouldn't be.

**Assumption that matters most**: "At scale, shipping per heavy order drops below margin." → Cheapest test: within 48 hours, get written quotes from 2–3 carriers or 3PLs for 15 kg parcels at current volume and at 10× volume, and put them into the per-order model. If even the 10× quote leaves contribution negative, volume is ruled out as the fix.

**Closest precedent**: **Pets.com**. You match it on market, model, mechanism and the marketing-first launch. You're *not* Pets.com only if you change the design the way Chewy did:
- recurring delivery
- a minimum order for free shipping
- a richer product mix
- prices at parity with supermarkets, not below them

**Levers that actually move contribution per order:**
- A free-shipping threshold, or a shipping fee on single heavy items.
- A subscription/autoship discount instead of an across-the-board price cut.
- Bundling heavy items with higher-margin ones (treats, accessories, own brand).
- Pickup points for single heavy bags.
- Regional launch to raise delivery density.
- Matching supermarket prices instead of undercutting them.

**Next step (48 h)**: Build a per-order contribution sheet for three baskets: a single 15 kg bag, food plus litter, and food plus treats/accessories. Include COGS, pick/pack, packaging, shipping, payment fees and damage/returns. Fill in real carrier quotes at 1× and 10× volume. Done when each basket shows a positive or negative contribution figure under each pricing and shipping option above.

**Kill criterion**: If the sheet shows no realistic pricing and shipping design with positive contribution on repeat orders, don't launch at all. Otherwise, if a 1–2 region pilot without TV (search and social only) doesn't reach positive contribution per order and at least an estimated 40% 90-day repeat rate by **31 March 2027**, don't commit the TV budget. Treat the 40% threshold as an estimate to tune against your own customer-acquisition cost.
