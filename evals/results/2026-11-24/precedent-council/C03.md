**Frame.** Decide whether to shut down the failing online game and turn the internal chat tool into the company's product, or keep the game, or find a third option, given that the chat tool is loved by "everyone who tries it."

- **Reversibility:** mixed. Shutting the game is close to a **one-way door**: players leave, the community breaks up and the team moves on. Testing the chat tool with outsiders is a **two-way door**.
- **Stakes:** runway, team, and whatever reputation you have with players and investors.
- **Horizon:** depends on runway. That's unknown.

**Assumptions (you didn't give numbers, so I'm stating them):** you're a small studio and the game is losing money. "Everyone who tries it" means your team plus a handful of friends or contacts, not paying outside teams. The chat tool has no revenue, no external users at scale and no sales motion. If any of these is wrong, especially if outside teams already use it weekly, the verdict moves toward "do it now."

## Precedents

No entry in my precedent library matches this decision on 2 or more dimensions. The library covers grocery delivery, subscriptions, hardware and streaming, so I don't cite any of it here. From well-established public history (not checked against sources in this run):

- ✓ **Slack (Tiny Speck, 2012–2013).** Its game *Glitch* failed and was shut down in late 2012. The internal chat tool the team had built while making the game became Slack. **Matches:** mechanism (failing game, loved internal chat tool), market (team communication), model (B2B, per-team). **Differs:** timing. In 2013 the market was fragmented (HipChat, Campfire, email), Microsoft Teams didn't exist yet, and Tiny Speck still had investor money when it pivoted. Before launch it tested the product with outside companies, not just its own staff.
- ✓ **Discord (Hammer & Chisel, ~2015).** A studio making a game (*Fates Forever*) built a chat product, which became the company. **Matches:** mechanism (game studio pivots to its communication tool), market (real-time chat). **Differs:** model (free consumer app, not B2B) and timing.
- ☠ **No failure that matches 2 or more dimensions** is in my library or something I can cite reliably. **This is a warning, not reassurance.** You hear about Slack and Discord because they won. The many studios that tried the same pivot and failed didn't make the news. Treat the two survivors as proof that the move *can* work, not that it usually does.

## Independent opinions

Seats: the 5 fixed seats, plus **Customer** (the whole question is whether outsiders want this) and **Finance** (shutting the game is a runway decision).

**1. Devil's advocate: Against (for now)**
The most fragile assumption is that "loved by everyone who tries it" means "people will switch to it and pay." Your testers are colleagues and friends. They're biased, they get free support and they have no switching cost. Outside teams already live in Slack, Teams, Google Chat or Discord, often bundled into suites they already pay for. Slack won a fragmented market in 2013. You'd be entering a consolidated one in 2026. A well-liked internal tool is a nice feature, not proof of a market. *Must be true for me to be wrong:* outside teams adopt it and keep using it without you pushing. **Confidence: medium-high.**

**2. First principles: Depends**
The real question isn't "game or chat." It's "where is there evidence of demand outside our building?" Right now there's none for either: the game has failed with outsiders, and the chat tool hasn't been tried by outsiders. Third option: **don't shut the game yet.** Freeze its development, keep the servers on at minimal cost, and give the chat tool one short test with outside teams. Also ask *why* your team loves it. If the answer is something specific (e.g. a feature built around how your team works), that's the product. "Chat" isn't. **(Opinion, built on the Slack pattern.) Confidence: high.**

**3. Expansionist: For**
This is the Slack pattern almost exactly. You built a tool to solve your own problem while doing something hard, and the people who use it every day love it. That's rarer than it sounds. Most B2B products start with no daily users. *Why you might not be the losers in this pattern:* you already have a working product and a team that understands it, and 2026 buyers are actively trying new collaboration tools. That last part is my opinion, not checked. Find the niche the giants serve badly (game studios, creative teams, a specific workflow) and you don't need to beat Slack head-on. *Must be true:* the love comes from something the incumbents lack, not from familiarity. **Confidence: medium.**

**4. Outsider: Depends**
If I'm a stranger, my first question is "why would I leave the chat app my whole company already uses?" "Our team loves it" doesn't answer that. I also don't know what it does differently, because you haven't said. If you can't explain it to me in one sentence, you aren't ready to sell it. And if you shut the game, what happens to your players? **(Opinion.) Confidence: medium.**

**5. Executor: Depends, leaning toward a staged move**
Tomorrow at 9:00: list 10–15 outside teams you can reach directly (other studios, agencies, former colleagues). Package the tool so a stranger can sign up without your help: setup, accounts and security basics. What will block you: chat is infrastructure, so companies will ask about data handling, uptime, SSO and compliance before they move real work onto it. You can make the tool self-serve in a few weeks. Enterprise-ready takes months. Keep the game running on minimal staffing until outside data exists. **(Opinion.) Confidence: medium-high.**

**6. Customer: Against shutting the game on current evidence**
A team lead who switches chat tools risks annoying the whole team, so they'll only do it for a clear, painful problem the current tool doesn't solve. Your internal users never had to make that switch: the tool was simply there. Slack's lesson is that it tested with outside companies before betting everything on it. **Confidence: medium.**

**7. Finance: Depends on runway**
If the game drains cash every month and has no plausible way to recover, running it at full cost is the worst option. Cut its spending now, whatever you decide about chat. But a B2B chat product earns little in its first months, and competitors give chat away in bundles, so pricing will be under pressure. Tiny Speck pivoted with investor money still in the bank. Work out how many months you can survive with *zero* chat revenue before you commit. **(Opinion; I don't have your numbers.) Confidence: medium.**

## Pre-mortem: 12 months later, you shut the game and the chat product failed. Why?

1. **The love didn't travel.** Outside teams tried it, liked it, then drifted back to the tool their company already used. *Early warning:* weekly active use by outside teams falls after week 2–3, or only one person per outside team uses it.
2. **Asked to "replace Slack/Teams."** Buyers compared you on features and on bundled price, and you lost on both. *Early warning:* your sales conversations keep ending in "we already have X." That's the trap Slack avoided by entering a fragmented market (Slack precedent).
3. **The runway ran out before revenue arrived.** The team spent months on security, SSO and integrations before anyone paid. *Early warning:* by month 3, no outside team has agreed to pay or signed a letter of intent.

## Chair's verdict

**Decision:** Shut the failing game and make the internal chat tool the product, or not.

**Verdict:** Do it like this. **Don't shut the game yet. Freeze it.** Cut it to minimum spend and staffing now, and run a 6-week test of the chat tool with outside teams. Only shut the game and commit fully if the test passes. · **Confidence: 7/10**

**Agreement:** The game shouldn't keep getting full investment. Internal love isn't evidence of demand. The Slack pattern is real but rare, and it involved outside testing before the bet.

**Real disagreement:** Whether a 2026 chat market dominated by bundled incumbents leaves room for a newcomer. The Expansionist says yes, in a niche. The Devil's advocate says prove it first. Only outside usage data settles this.

**Assumption that matters most:** That outside teams, not just your colleagues, will adopt it and keep using it without your help. → **Cheapest test:** put it in front of 10 outside teams for 6 weeks and measure weekly active use per team. Also ask each team lead: "Would you pay €X per user to keep it?"

**Closest precedent:** **Slack.** You match it on mechanism, market and model. You may not match it on timing (a consolidated market vs a fragmented one) or on outside validation (Slack had it before going all in). You can check the second this month. Accept the first by choosing a niche.

**Next step (48 h):** Write down the one-sentence reason your team prefers this tool to Slack/Teams. Then send a personal invitation to 15 outside teams (studios, agencies, contacts) and aim to get 10 onboarded by the end of week 2. In parallel, set the game's budget to maintenance-only.

**Kill criterion:** These thresholds are my suggestions, so adjust them to your runway. If by week 6 fewer than 4 of the 10 outside teams use it weekly with most of their members, or none would pay, don't pivot into generic chat. Either narrow to the specific feature people loved, or put the effort elsewhere. If 6 or more teams are active and at least 2 would pay, shut the game properly (tell players, offer refunds or an export where relevant) and commit.
