## Step 0 · Frame

**Decide whether to double the €19-first-cleaning discount budget, or hold or redesign it, when many discounted customers book once and then hire the cleaner directly, off the platform.**

- **Reversibility**: Mostly a two-way door, because you can cut the budget next month. But the money you spend is gone for good. A €19 anchor also attracts deal-seekers, and their expectations are hard to reset later.
- **Stakes**: Mainly cash and runway. Also the trust of your freelancer supply and, in the EU, how your freelancers are legally classified.
- **Horizon**: Not given. I assume this is a next-budget-cycle decision (weeks, not days).

**Assumptions** (you didn't give these numbers, so I'm making them explicit):
- The cleaner is paid about €60 per job and the platform keeps about €20 (a ~25% take). **Estimate.**
- So each discounted first cleaning costs you about **−€41 in contribution** (€19 in, €60 out), before ad spend and payment fees.
- Each repeat cleaning booked through the platform earns about **+€20**.
- **Estimate:** a discounted customer needs at least ~2 repeat bookings on the platform just to pay back the discount.
- "Many" customers leave after one booking. You haven't measured the repeat rate per cohort, meaning per group of customers acquired in the same period (I assume this).

## Step 2 · Independent opinions

Specialist seats: **Finance**, because this is a contribution-margin question. **Risk & compliance**, because the usual fixes for leakage (exclusivity, controlling cleaners) push freelancers toward legally looking like employees in the EU.

**A · Devil's advocate**
- **Position:** Against.
- **Strongest argument:** You're running MoviePass economics with a leak in the bucket. Every €19 booking loses about €41 (est.) and then hands the customer a trusted cleaner they can hire directly. Doubling the budget doubles the number of free introductions you pay for. The deal-seekers come first, just as MoviePass's heaviest users did.
- **Most fragile assumption:** that the people who stay on the platform will cover the losses on the ones who leave.
- **What would have to be true for "against" to be wrong:** discounted cohorts already average 3+ on-platform cleans within 90 days.
- **Confidence:** High.

**B · First principles**
- **Position:** Against as framed. Third option: change *what* you discount.
- **The real question:** Why would anyone stay after meeting the cleaner? Right now the app's value ends at the introduction.
  - The customer stays only if the platform does something the direct deal can't: a substitute when the cleaner is sick, insurance or damage cover, no cash handling, flexible rescheduling, invoices.
  - The cleaner stays only if the platform is cheaper for them than going direct: lower commission on repeat clients, guaranteed payment, a full schedule.
- **Third option:** Tie the discount to staying. For example, €19 only when the customer books a recurring biweekly plan, or €20 off each of the first 3 cleans.
- **What would have to be true:** customers value those services above the commission. (opinion)
- **Confidence:** Medium.

**C · Expansionist**
- **Position:** Depends.
- **Why you're not automatically MoviePass:** MoviePass lost more the more people used it. Your repeat cleans *earn* about €20 each (est.). Your loss is a one-time acquisition cost, not an ongoing one, which makes you closer to Instacart than to MoviePass.
- **The upside:** Density. More customers per neighbourhood means fuller cleaner schedules and less cleaner churn, and that's a real moat in local services.
- **Condition:** Double the budget only in neighbourhoods where repeat data already shows payback.
- **What would have to be true:** at least one cohort or area already recovers the €41 within 90 days.
- **Confidence:** Medium.

**D · Outsider**
- **Position:** Against.
- **Argument:** As a customer, €19 says "this is what cleaning is worth", and €80 afterwards feels like a 4x price rise. Of course I ask the nice cleaner "can I just pay you directly?". She probably charges me €60–65, and we both win. Your discount teaches me the cleaner is good and your app is expensive. (opinion, but it follows directly from the leakage you reported)
- **What would have to be true for doubling to work:** the jump from €19 to €80 doesn't scare people off.
- **Confidence:** Medium.

**E · Executor**
- **Position:** Against for now.
- **Tomorrow at 9:00:** Can you even produce a table of discounted cohorts showing the % who make a 2nd *on-platform* booking within 30, 60 and 90 days? If not, doubling the budget means spending blind.
- **The blocker:** You can't easily measure leakage itself (bookings you never see). Proxy it with cleaners whose bookings from a given client stop while the cleaner stays active, plus a short exit survey.
- **What would have to be true:** that data exists within a week.
- **Confidence:** High.

**F · Finance (specialist)**
- **Position:** Against.
- **The arithmetic (all estimates):**
  - CAC, the cost to acquire a customer = €41 subsidy + any ad spend.
  - Payback needs ≥2 on-platform repeats at ~€20 each.
  - If, say, 30% of customers rebook and those average 4 more cleans, the average customer brings 1.2 repeats ≈ €24. That loses about €17 per customer, before ads.
  - Doubling the budget doubles that loss.
- **Grounding:** Webvan's lesson: prove the per-unit economics in one market before scaling the spend.
- **What would have to be true:** average repeats per acquired customer ≥ 2.5 (buffer for fees and ads).
- **Confidence:** High on the logic, low on the numbers (they're assumed).

**G · Risk & compliance (specialist)**
- **Position:** Depends on *how* you fight leakage.
- **The risk:** The obvious fixes (no-poach clauses, exclusivity, penalising cleaners, setting their prices) increase the control you exercise over the freelancers. Under the EU Platform Work Directive (2024), member states must create a legal presumption of employment based on platform control; national rules are due by late 2026. In Spain there is also the existing "falso autónomo" (false self-employment) doctrine.
- **Precedent:** The labor-model fight is what reportedly finished Homejoy.
- **Better route:** Incentives (lower repeat commission, benefits) rather than restrictions.
- **What would have to be true:** your counsel confirms where your control level sits.
- **Confidence:** Medium.

## Step 5 · Pre-mortem

*It's 12 months later and the chosen option failed. Why?*

1. **The discount funded leakage.**
   - Customers acquired at a loss moved off-platform after 1–2 cleans.
   - **Early signal:** 60-day on-platform rebooking falls as the discount volume grows, and cleaners stay active while their regular clients disappear.
   - **Precedent:** MoviePass (losses rose with growth). Homejoy (reported weak repeat usage).
2. **Burn outran the proof.**
   - New cities or areas were opened on discounts before any one of them paid back.
   - **Early signal:** blended CAC payback rises above ~6 months (estimate), with no area clearly profitable.
   - **Precedent:** Webvan expanding while San Francisco was "wobbly".
3. **The anti-leakage fix backfired.**
   - Restrictions on cleaners triggered complaints, cleaner churn, or a classification challenge.
   - **Early signal:** cleaner churn up after policy changes, or legal letters.
   - **Precedent:** Homejoy (labor lawsuits). EU Platform Work Directive exposure.

## Step 6 · Chair's verdict

**Decision**: Whether to double the €19 first-cleaning budget while discounted customers are leaking off-platform.

**Verdict**: **Don't double it. Hold the budget flat and do this instead:**
1. Measure on-platform payback per cohort.
2. Redesign the offer so the discount rewards staying, not the introduction. Examples: €19 only with a recurring plan, or the discount spread over the first 3 cleans.
3. Give cleaners an incentive (not a restriction) to keep repeat clients on the app.
4. Then increase the budget only in areas that clear payback.

· **Confidence: 8/10**

**Agreement**: Six of seven seats agree on the core point: with leakage this visible, more subsidy buys more free introductions for your cleaners, not more customers for you. Even the Expansionist only argues for *reallocating* the budget, not doubling it.

**Real disagreement**: How much density matters before leakage is fixed. The Expansionist says acquisition can't stop or supply churns. Finance and the Devil's advocate say density without retention benefits the cleaners, not the platform.

**Assumption that matters most**: Discounted customers book enough repeat cleans *on the platform* to recover the subsidy. By estimate that's ≥2 repeats at ~€20 contribution each, to cover a ~€41 first-clean loss.
- **Cheapest test:** a cohort query on data you already have. For discounted customers, % with a 2nd on-platform booking, and average on-platform cleans at 30, 60 and 90 days, split by area.
- **Second test:** a 2-week A/B. Arm A: €19 one-off. Arm B: €19 only with a biweekly plan, or €20 off each of the first 3 cleans. Compare on 60-day on-platform cleans per € of subsidy.

**Closest precedent**:
- **Homejoy** (general knowledge, to verify): the same market and the same discount-and-leakage pattern.
- **MoviePass** for the mechanism.
- **Why you aren't MoviePass yet:** your repeat jobs make money, so the loss is a one-time acquisition cost that *can* be recovered. That's only true if customers stay on the app, and you've told us many don't.

**Next step (48 h)**: Pull the discounted-cohort table: on-platform repeat rate and average cleans per customer at 30, 60 and 90 days, by area. Also count cleaners who are still active but whose discounted clients stopped booking (a proxy for leakage). Put your real cleaner payout and take rate into the payback formula in place of my estimates.

**Kill criterion**: If, by **8 weeks** after launching the redesigned offer, discounted customers average **fewer than 3 on-platform cleans within 90 days** (first clean + 2 repeats, the estimated break-even), stop the first-clean discount entirely. Move the money to cleaner retention and referral, priced at full or near-full rate.
