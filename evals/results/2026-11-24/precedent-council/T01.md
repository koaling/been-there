**Decision**: Decide whether to build our own automated warehouses in 8 cities at once, or prove the model in one city first, given a large new round and a first city that loses about €4 per order.

- **Reversibility**: one-way door. Automated warehouses mean long leases, capex and automation contracts that you can't cheaply unwind.
- **Stakes**: most of the round, the company's runway, and your credibility with investors for the next raise.
- **Horizon**: before you sign any warehouse or automation contracts.

**Assumptions** (you didn't give these, so I'm going ahead without them):
1. The €4 is an average loss per order in city 1, and I don't know how much of it is variable (picking, delivery, packaging, discounts) versus fixed (rent, staff, the facility) spread over each order.
2. City 1 hasn't hit a steady order volume yet.
3. "Competitors" means other well-funded quick-commerce or grocery-delivery players in the same cities.

## Precedents

☠ **Webvan (1996–2001)**. Died of premature scaling and bad unit economics. It signed a ~$1B contract to build automated warehouses (~$30–35M each) for a 26-city plan and expanded while San Francisco was still unprofitable. The warehouses were sized well above the orders that came in, and delivery density per route never got high enough. **Matches: market, business model, mechanism, and context (big money, scaling before profit).** Differs: era (1999 vs today's smartphones and payments) and scale (8 cities, not 26). This is about as close as a precedent gets.

☠ **MoviePass (2017–2019)**. Died of pricing and unit economics: it lost money on every heavy user, and its parent company said monthly losses would *grow* as the service got more popular. **Matches: mechanism ("volume will fix it" when each unit loses money) and context (funded growth into negative unit economics).** Differs: market (cinema) and model (subscription). I use it only for one point: volume fixes fixed-cost losses, but it multiplies per-unit losses.

☠ **Gorillas / Getir quick-commerce wave (2020–2024)**. From general knowledge, not the precedent library, and stated without figures. These companies raised large rounds, opened dark stores in many European cities at the same time to beat competitors, then consolidated (Getir bought Gorillas in 2022) and pulled out of several markets (Getir left the UK, Germany and the Netherlands in 2024). **Matches: market, model (own fulfilment sites), context (land-grab funding), mechanism.** Differs: dark stores rather than automated warehouses, so they had less capex at risk than you will.

✓ **Instacart**. Survived the same grocery economics by owning no warehouses, trucks or inventory. Shoppers picked orders in existing supermarkets, so a new city meant recruiting shoppers and digitising local stores, not building infrastructure. Retailers became paying partners. **Matches: market, mechanism.** Differs: asset-light model.

✓ **Ocado**. From general knowledge, not the library. It is the counter-example for owning automated warehouses: it did win with them, but it grew one warehouse at a time, sourced through a retail partner (Waitrose) at first, and took many years to become profitable. **Matches: market, model, mechanism.** Differs: it went step by step, not 8 cities at once.

Considered and dropped: Quibi (big raise, but the failure mechanism differs) and Juicero (overengineering, but a different market and model). Neither matches on 2 dimensions in a useful way.

## Independent opinions

**1. Devil's advocate — Against · Confidence: high**
This is Webvan's plan with a smaller number. The weakest assumption is "volume should fix that." Volume only fixes the fixed-cost part of the €4. If picking plus last-mile costs more than basket margin plus delivery fee, every extra order adds to the loss, which is exactly how MoviePass died. Eight cities also split your volume eight ways, so each warehouse gets less density, while density is the one thing that drives delivery cost down. Webvan's warehouses ran far below capacity.
*Would have to be true:* the €4 is almost all fixed-cost absorption, and each city will reach break-even volume within your runway.

**2. First principles — Depends · Confidence: medium**
The real problem isn't "be in 8 cities first." It's "own a market where customers order often and each order pays for itself." Automation lowers picking cost but does nothing for last-mile cost, which in same-day delivery usually depends on drops per route. Third option: one automated site in city 1 (or a manual pilot to prove the throughput assumptions), plus asset-light entry (store-picking or partner micro-fulfilment) in 2–3 more cities to reserve the brand and learn local demand. Instacart showed that you can enter cities without building anything.
*Would have to be true:* demand can be tested without owning the warehouse.

**3. Expansionist — For, with conditions · Confidence: low–medium**
Grocery delivery does reward scale: procurement terms, brand, and fixed costs spread over more orders. Ocado shows that owned automation can become a moat. And unlike 1999, customers already order groceries on their phones (opinion, but widely observed). If a competitor locks in landlords, suppliers and habits first, coming second costs more. You aren't Webvan only if city 1 shows positive contribution at a reachable volume and the automation is modular, so capacity can grow in step with demand rather than ahead of it.
*Would have to be true:* the €4 shrinks as volume grows in city 1, as shown in data, not just forecast.

**4. Outsider — Against · Confidence: medium**
From the outside: you lose money on every order and want to do it in eight places at once. As a customer I don't care whether the warehouse is automated. I care about price, whether my items are in stock, and the delivery window. If you raise fees to fix the €4, will I still order? Nobody has tested that yet (opinion).
*Would have to be true:* customers in city 1 keep ordering at a price that covers the cost.

**5. Executor — Against 8 now · Confidence: high**
Eight automated warehouses means eight sites, eight permit processes, eight hiring drives and eight automation commissioning runs, all running in parallel while your team is still learning how to run city 1. Automation projects typically take many months from contract to stable throughput (estimate). Tomorrow at 9:00 the work that can actually happen is: break the €4 into its parts, model break-even orders per day, and test pricing in city 1. A slipped automation vendor timeline would block everything else.
*Would have to be true:* you have proven ops leaders for 8 cities and a vendor with multi-site delivery track record.

**Specialist: Finance — Against · Confidence: high** (picked because the case depends on per-order economics and runway)
Make one split: contribution margin per order (basket margin + fees − picking − packaging − delivery − payment − promotions) versus fixed cost per order. If contribution is negative, the plan's core premise is false and volume makes the loss worse. If it's positive and only fixed costs cause the €4, calculate the orders per day that breaks even and ask whether city 1 can reach it. Building 8 sites before you know which case you're in bets the round on an unchecked spreadsheet line.
*Would have to be true:* positive contribution per order today.

## Pre-mortem: it's November 2027 and the 8-city build failed. Why?

1. **The €4 was mostly variable cost, so volume made the loss worse.**
   *Early signal:* loss per order in city 1 doesn't fall as weekly orders grow.
   *Precedent:* MoviePass's parent said losses would grow with popularity.
2. **The warehouses ran far below capacity, so fixed costs were never covered.**
   *Early signal:* utilisation in the first 2–3 sites stays well below plan by month 3 after opening.
   *Precedent:* Webvan's warehouses were sized well above actual daily orders.
3. **Too few drops per route outside the core neighbourhoods.**
   *Early signal:* deliveries per route-hour flatten as you add postcodes.
   *Precedent:* Webvan's delivery density per route never got high enough.

## Chair's verdict

**Decision**: Build automated warehouses in 8 cities now, or prove the per-order economics first.
**Verdict**: Don't build 8 now. First find out whether the €4 is a volume problem or a per-order problem, and prove it in city 1. · Confidence: 8/10

**Agreement**: Every seat except the Expansionist is against committing to 8 automated warehouses now. Even the Expansionist makes its case conditional on proof from city 1.

**Real disagreement**: How much speed matters against competitors. The chair's answer: secure sites with options or letters of intent, and maybe enter 1–2 cities asset-light, but keep automation capex tied to the city-1 numbers.

**Assumption that matters most**: "Volume will fix the €4." → Cheapest test: build a per-order cost waterfall for city 1 that splits variable and fixed costs. If contribution per order is negative, volume can't fix it. If it's positive, compute break-even orders per day and compare it with your real growth curve. This takes a few days and costs nothing.

**Closest precedent**: Webvan. It had the same market, the same model (own automated warehouses), the same move (many cities funded by a big raise while the first city lost money) and the same promise that volume would fix it. You aren't Webvan only if city 1 shows positive contribution per order and a falling loss curve before you commit the capex. Today nothing you've told me shows that.

**Next step (48 h)**: Produce the city-1 per-order waterfall from the last 8 weeks of real orders: basket margin, fees, picking, packaging, delivery, payment, promotions, and fixed cost per order. Mark each line variable or fixed, and compute contribution per order and break-even orders per day.

**Kill criterion**: If contribution per order in city 1 isn't positive (≥ €0 before fixed costs) by **31 May 2027**, or the fully loaded loss per order isn't clearly falling as volume grows, don't sign any additional warehouse or automation contracts. Change the model instead (pricing, minimum basket, delivery windows, or asset-light fulfilment).
