## Step 0 · Frame

**Decision**: Launch always-on-camera smart glasses to the general public at €1,400 now, or hold or reshape the launch, given that testers love them but bystanders feel uneasy and some bars already ban them.

- **Reversibility**: mostly a **one-way door**. A product can be withdrawn, but a public reputation as "the creepy glasses" is very hard to undo, and so are venue bans once they spread.
- **Stakes**: reputation (brand and category), money (inventory, marketing), regulatory exposure (EU GDPR and national data-protection authorities, venue and workplace rules), and people (bystanders who never agreed to anything).
- **Horizon**: assumed to be a launch planned within the next few months.

**Assumptions** (no questions asked, so I'm stating them):
1. The camera can record without a strong, tamper-resistant signal that bystanders notice.
2. The "early testers" are self-selected enthusiasts, not a random sample of buyers.
3. You sell in the EU, so GDPR and EU data-protection authorities matter.
4. The glasses look noticeably "tech" rather than like ordinary eyewear (this changes the outcome; see below).

## Step 1 · Precedents

The local precedent library has **no entry tagged `trust-privacy` or smart glasses**. Juicero (consumer hardware, priced high) matches on market only. Its failure mechanism was different (the hardware wasn't needed), so I'm not citing it as a core precedent. The cases below come from well-established public history, not from the library, and they still need sources before anyone relies on them in a document.

```
Precedents
☠ Google Glass Explorer Edition (2013–2015): consumer version halted; died of trust-privacy + pricing (~$1,500).
  Matches: market (consumer smart glasses), model (premium hardware sale), mechanism (bystander rejection, venue bans,
  the "Glasshole" label). Differs: timing (2013 had weaker AI use cases and less normalised wearables).
☠ Snap Spectacles v1 (2016–2017): far cheaper (~$130), but Snap wrote down unsold inventory.
  Matches: market, mechanism (camera-on-face novelty that faded). Differs: price, and its main problem was retention
  more than bystander backlash.
✓ Ray-Ban Meta smart glasses (2023–): the same "camera on your face" trap, survived by looking like ordinary Ray-Bans,
  a visible recording LED, a price around a fifth of yours (~€300+), distribution through optical retail, and
  photos/audio/AI marketed instead of "recording".
✓ Google Glass Enterprise Edition (2017–2023): survived the consumer backlash for years by moving to workplaces
  (logistics, manufacturing, medicine), where wearers and the people around them are colleagues who agreed to it.
```

Your situation is closest to Google Glass: the price, the bans and the bystander discomfort all match, and all three appeared before mass launch.

## Step 2 · Independent opinions

Specialist seats: **Risk & compliance** (bystander recording under GDPR and EU DPA scrutiny) and **Customer** (the people who suffer the downside are not the buyers).

**1. Devil's advocate**: *Against.* The fragile assumption is that tester enthusiasm predicts mass adoption. Glass testers loved it too. What killed it was social veto by non-users: bans in bars, cinemas and workplaces, plus a slur for the people wearing it. At €1,400 you are selling a status object, and a status object that gets you asked to leave a bar loses its status. The bans you already see are the same early warning signal Glass showed. *Must be true for me to be wrong:* bystander discomfort fades with exposure instead of hardening into norms. *Confidence: high.*

**2. First principles**: *Depends.* What is the job? Testers probably love hands-free capture, AI assistance and staying heads-up, not the "always-available camera" as such. Third option: launch with the camera **off by default and physically signalled** (an LED that the camera cannot work without, or a hardware shutter), or launch a camera-less or audio/AI-first version to consumers and keep the full camera for consenting contexts. *Must be true:* most of the value survives a camera constraint. Test this. *Confidence: medium.*

**3. Expansionist**: *For, with conditions.* This is not 2013. Ray-Ban Meta shows the public will accept camera glasses when they look normal, signal recording and are priced like eyewear. Smartphones themselves went through the same "everyone can film you" panic and became normal. The upside is owning the premium tier before it consolidates. But €1,400 is your Glass-like trait: the survivor won on price and design, not on features. *Must be true:* your product is clearly better than the ~€300 normal-looking alternative in a way buyers will pay four to five times more for. *Confidence: medium.*

**4. Outsider**: *Against, for now.* What I hear as a normal person is "€1,400 glasses that people in bars don't let in." Why would I pay that much to be unwelcome? And the people near me can't tell whether I'm filming them. If I can't explain to my friends why it's fine, I won't wear it around them. *(opinion)* *Confidence: medium.*

**5. Executor**: *Depends.* Tomorrow at 9:00 you need retail partners, a returns policy, support scripts for "my glasses were banned" complaints, and a press plan that will certainly be asked about privacy. Blocker: you have no answer yet that a journalist or a bar owner would accept. A full launch without one means the first headlines set the narrative, as they did for Glass. A staged launch (one city, invite-only, venue partnerships) can be done with current resources. *Confidence: medium.*

**6. Risk & compliance**: *Against launching unchanged.* Under GDPR the wearer's purely personal use may fall under the household exemption, but you as the manufacturer are a controller for anything processed in your cloud or AI pipeline, including faces of non-consenting third parties. EU DPAs have questioned camera glasses before (as I recall, Italy's Garante raised questions about Ray-Ban Stories' recording indicator in 2021; to verify). Expect a DPIA, scrutiny of the recording indicator, possible face-recognition limits under the AI Act, and venue and workplace bans that you can't control. *Must be true for "go":* tamper-proof indicator, on-device processing by default, no face identification. *Confidence: medium-high.*

**7. Customer**: *Depends.* You have two customers: the buyer (happy) and everyone around the buyer (unhappy, with veto power through social pressure and bans). Your testing only measured the first group. *Must be true:* tested with bystanders, not just wearers, and the discomfort is clearly falling across repeated exposure. *Confidence: medium.*

There's real disagreement: A (against, high) vs C (for with conditions, medium).

**A rebuts C**: "Ray-Ban Meta is a survivor *because* it isn't you: under €400, indistinguishable from normal glasses, an LED and optical-store distribution. The answer is to fix the bystander experience and the entry price, not to abandon consumers. A delay just hands the premium tier to Meta and Apple-class competitors."

Chair notes: both agree that **the current configuration** shouldn't launch broadly. They disagree only on whether consumers are the right market eventually.

## Step 5 · Pre-mortem (12 months after a full consumer launch, it failed)

1. **Social veto spread faster than adoption.** Bans moved from a few bars to chains, gyms, cinemas, schools and offices, and wearers stopped wearing them in public.
   *Early signal*: the number of venues with bans and the share of buyers' daily wear time dropping after week 4. *Precedent*: Google Glass (bans and the "Glasshole" label appeared within months of the Explorer rollout).
2. **The price was set for enthusiasts, not the mass market.** After the early adopters bought, demand stalled against a ~€300 normal-looking alternative.
   *Early signal*: conversion collapsing after the waitlist is exhausted, pressure to discount within the first quarter. *Precedent*: Glass's ~$1,500 price; the price-cut pattern seen with Juicero (library), though Juicero's cause was different.
3. **A privacy incident or regulator action set the story.** A viral clip of covert recording, or a data-protection authority inquiry into the indicator or the cloud processing, led to forced changes after launch.
   *Early signal*: the first press question about covert recording that you can't answer with a hardware fact, or a complaint filed with a data-protection authority. *Precedent*: the Garante questions on Ray-Ban Stories (to verify).

## Step 6 · Chair's verdict

**Decision**: Launch always-on-camera glasses to the general public at €1,400 now.
**Verdict**: **Don't launch to the mass market now. First find out whether bystander acceptance can be fixed in hardware**, through a staged, invite-only launch with a tamper-proof recording signal. · Confidence: **7/10**

**Agreement**: The current configuration matches the Google Glass failure on every dimension that killed it: high price, visible tech, covert-capable camera, early venue bans. Tester love is real but measured on the wrong population.
**Real disagreement**: Whether consumers are the right market at all (C, E) or whether this should start where consent is built in (Glass Enterprise path, partly A and F).
**Assumption that matters most**: *Bystander discomfort falls with familiarity and a clear recording signal.* → Cheapest test: put 30–50 units with a hardware recording indicator (one the camera can't operate without) into everyday use in one city for 6–8 weeks. Survey the **people around wearers** (colleagues, friends, venue staff) at week 1 and week 6, and track whether wearers still wear them in public.
**Closest precedent**: **Google Glass Explorer Edition**. Today you are Glass. To become Ray-Ban Meta you would need to look normal, signal recording clearly and cut the entry price sharply, or justify the premium with a feature the cheap alternative lacks.

**Next step (48 h)**: Write a one-page "bystander promise" with hardware facts only (indicator that can't be disabled, on-device processing by default, no face identification, audible or visible capture cue), and show it to 5 bar or restaurant owners in your area, including at least one that has a ban. Record whether any would lift or not impose the ban based on it.
**Kill criterion**: if, by the end of the 8-week pilot, bystander discomfort has not fallen by at least a third between week 1 and week 6, **or** more than 30% of pilot wearers have stopped wearing them in public, drop the mass-consumer launch and redirect to consent-based settings (enterprise, accessibility, sport, creators with model releases) or to a camera-optional consumer model.

▸ Council

- **Outsider**: Against for now. "€1,400 to be unwelcome" is an impossible pitch.
