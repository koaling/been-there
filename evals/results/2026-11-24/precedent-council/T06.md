**Decision**: Decide whether to open central kitchens in 3 more cities now, or first fix waste and delivery economics where you already operate, given ~20% food waste and delivery costs that take most of the margin.

- **Reversibility**: mostly a one-way door. Kitchen leases, fit-outs, equipment and local teams are expensive to unwind.
- **Stakes**: capital (probably the largest spend in the company's history), runway, team bandwidth.
- **Horizon**: not given. I assume no external deadline forces the decision.

**Assumptions** (no clarifying questions in this run): you run kitchens in 1 or 2 cities today. No city is clearly profitable at contribution level after delivery and waste. "Reach scale" means the hope that more volume will fix the margin. Customers order on demand, the same day.

## Precedents

The library had no direct match for a chef-meal company with central kitchens. The closest entries come from grocery delivery that owned its own infrastructure.

```
☠ Webvan (1996-2001): died of premature scaling and unit economics. It built ~$30-35M warehouses for a 26-city plan and moved into new cities while San Francisco was still unprofitable. Warehouses were sized well above actual orders, and delivery density per route never got high enough.
  Matches: business model (own infrastructure + own delivery), mechanism (expanding before one-city economics work), market (perishable food delivered to homes). Differs: timing (dot-com capital) and scale of capex (yours is far smaller).
☠ Munchery (US, ~2010-2019): chef-prepared meals cooked in its own central kitchens and delivered. It expanded beyond San Francisco to other US cities, pulled out of them in 2018 and shut down in early 2019. [Not in the precedent library.   Matches: market, business model, mechanism. Differs: timing (VC-funded era of meal delivery).
✓ Instacart: survived the same grocery-delivery economics by owning no warehouses, trucks or inventory. It used existing stores and shoppers' own vehicles, so a new city meant recruiting shoppers and onboarding stores, not building infrastructure.
```

## Independent opinions

**1. Devil's advocate: Against · high confidence**
The most likely way this fails is the Webvan pattern: infrastructure for many cities before one works. Your two problems are per-kitchen and per-route problems. Waste comes from cooking before demand is known, which happens in every kitchen. Delivery cost comes from route density, which is a property of each city. Three more cities do not fix either one. They copy both, and each new city starts with lower density than your current one. The weakest assumption is "scale fixes margin." Scale helps purchasing and overhead, not the two costs you named.
*What would have to be true:* a new city reaches profitable density faster than your current one did. You have no evidence of that.

**2. First principles: Against, with a third option · medium confidence**
The real problem is not reach. It is that you produce against a forecast and deliver on demand, which is the most expensive combination. Third option: become order-driven before you expand. Use pre-orders with a cutoff (the evening before, or a weekly plan), cook mostly to order, and deliver in fixed time windows by zone so routes are batched. You could also add pickup points such as offices, gyms or lockers. Once waste is close to zero and drops per route are high, expansion becomes a copy of a working machine. *(opinion, but consistent with the Webvan lesson)*
*What would have to be true:* enough customers accept planning ahead.

**3. Expansionist: Depends · medium confidence**
You are not Webvan. Kitchen capex is a fraction of an automated warehouse, and Instacart shows the trap can be avoided by not owning the heavy part. The upside: if the format works, cities are where the value is, and a competitor could take them first. The way to do this without being Webvan is to enter new cities asset-light: rented time in shared or dark kitchens, or a partner caterer cooking your recipes, plus third-party or pickup logistics. Then scale only where pre-orders prove demand.
*What would have to be true:* your margin problem is mostly fixed overhead that volume dilutes, not variable cost per order. You have not shown that.

**4. Outsider: Against · medium confidence**
From the outside this sounds like: "we lose money on each meal, so let's make more meals in more places." One in five meals ends up in the bin, which customers would also dislike if they knew. As a customer, I would happily order the night before for a discount or a guaranteed slot. Nobody seems to have asked customers. *(opinion)*
*What would have to be true:* customers really need same-day ordering. That is testable.

**5. Executor: Against expansion now; for an operations sprint · high confidence**
Tomorrow at 9:00 an expansion means finding sites, negotiating leases, hiring chefs and drivers, getting permits and health inspections in 3 cities, while the current kitchen still wastes 20%. Management attention is the real bottleneck. What is doable tomorrow: pull 8-12 weeks of order data per dish, day and zone. Measure waste per dish. Calculate cost per delivery per zone. Launch a pre-order cutoff in one zone.
*What would block it:* no clean data per order and per route.

**6. Finance (specialist: the question is unit economics): Against · high confidence**
Food cost is the biggest variable cost in meal businesses. A 20% waste rate inflates effective food cost by about 25% (1/0.8, rough estimate). Add delivery costs that "eat most of the margin" and contribution per order is likely close to zero or negative. In that case, expansion multiplies the loss and adds fixed costs (rent, kitchen staff) in each city, each with a ramp-up period. Before any capex, you need contribution margin per order to be positive in the current city, plus a payback period per kitchen.
*What would have to be true for "for":* contribution is already positive and only fixed costs are the problem. Your description suggests otherwise.

**7. Customer (specialist: the fix depends on ordering behaviour): Depends · medium confidence**
The whole fix depends on whether your customers will pre-order. Office lunches and families planning the week usually can. Impulse dinner buyers usually can't. Segment your current orders. If a large share is already repeat, predictable or B2B, you can move them to subscriptions or cutoffs. *(opinion; testable in days)*

## Pre-mortem: it's November 2027 and the 3-city expansion failed. Why?

1. **New cities never reached delivery density.** Orders were spread thin, and cost per drop stayed above the order margin.
   *Early signal:* drops per route-hour in the new city are below the current city at the same age. (Webvan: density never became high enough.)
2. **Waste rose instead of falling.** New cities have no demand history, so forecasts were worse and waste went above 20%.
   *Early signal:* weekly waste % in new kitchens trends above the original kitchen.
3. **Cash and management stretched across 4 cities.** The original city deteriorated while leadership opened the others, and runway ran out before any city broke even.
   *Early signal:* pulling out of a city within 12 months, which Munchery did in 2018 before closing (unverified, see above). Also: the original city's metrics slipping during the build-out. (Webvan: expanded while San Francisco was still "wobbly".)

## Chair's verdict

**Decision**: Open kitchens in 3 more cities now, or fix waste and delivery economics first.
**Verdict**: Don't expand now. Do it like this: switch to order-driven production and batched delivery in your current city, prove a positive contribution margin per order, then expand asset-light (rented or shared kitchen capacity, partner logistics) one city at a time. · Confidence: 8/10

**Agreement**: All seats agree that "scale" does not fix the two costs you named. Waste and delivery cost are local, per-kitchen and per-route problems, and expansion copies them.
**Real disagreement**: Only about timing and form. The Expansionist and Customer seats see a viable path to expansion once pre-orders and density work, and they favour asset-light entry. No one supports expanding with owned kitchens now.
**Assumption that matters most**: Enough of your customers will order ahead (cutoff the night before, or a weekly plan) in exchange for a discount or a guaranteed slot. That lets you cook close to demand and batch routes.
→ **Cheapest test**: offer a pre-order cutoff with a small discount (estimate: 10%) and fixed delivery windows in one zone for 3-4 weeks. Measure the adoption rate, waste % and cost per drop against a control zone.
**Closest precedent**: Webvan. You share the mechanism: owned infrastructure, expanding before one city works, and density as the binding constraint. You are not Webvan in capex size, so you can still change course cheaply, as long as you don't sign 3 leases first.

**Next step (48 h)**: Build a one-page unit-economics sheet for your current operation from the last 8-12 weeks. It should show revenue per order, food cost including waste, packaging, delivery cost per order by zone, and drops per route-hour. Then pick the zone for the pre-order pilot.
**Kill criterion**: Expand only if all three of these hold by end of March 2027:
- waste is below 8%
- delivery cost is below an agreed % of order value
- contribution margin per order is positive for 8 straight weeks

If pre-order adoption in the pilot zone is below ~30% of orders after 4 weeks, the order-driven model doesn't fit your customers. In that case reconsider the format itself (for example B2B or office lunch, or pickup points) rather than the number of cities. The 8% and 30% thresholds are estimates; adjust them to your own cost structure.
