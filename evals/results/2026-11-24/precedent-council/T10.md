**Frame.** Decide whether to scale a fully integrated construction model (design + factory components + materials supply + on-site building) across many projects at once, or something narrower, given large funding and first projects that ran late and over budget.

- **Reversibility:** one-way door. Factories, supply contracts and a multi-project backlog are hard to unwind.
- **Stakes:** very high. Capital, reputation with clients and lenders, people, and contractual liability for delays and defects.
- **Horizon:** now, before you commit to more factories or more concurrent projects.

**Assumptions (you didn't give numbers, so I'm assuming):** (1) you haven't yet shown a project where the integrated model beat a conventional build on cost and schedule; (2) "many projects at once" means several regions and building types; (3) the overruns on the first projects haven't been traced to root causes yet. If any of these is wrong, the verdict softens. See the kill criterion.

### Independent opinions

Specialist seats: **Finance** (factory utilisation and working capital decide this) and **Technical/Operations** (overruns usually come from design changes and on-site interfaces, not from who owns the supplier).

**1. Devil's advocate: Against · High.**
You're about to replay Katerra almost line for line: integration, many projects, large funding, early projects late and over budget. The most fragile assumption is that ownership fixes delays. Owning the chain also means owning every failure in it, plus a factory's fixed costs while projects slip. Your first projects already show the system doesn't work yet. Scaling it multiplies the losses.
*Must be true for me to be wrong:* the overruns were caused by outside suppliers and subcontractors, and the integrated version on a real project already ran on time.

**2. First principles: Depends · Medium.**
The real problem isn't who owns the chain. It's variability: one-off designs, late changes, poor sequencing. Factories only pay off with repetition. Third option: freeze a small catalogue of standard building types, own the design system and the factory for those only, and buy everything else. Integrate where repetition exists. Contract out where it doesn't.
*Must be true:* enough clients will accept standard designs.

**3. Expansionist: For, with conditions · Medium.**
Integration is a real advantage once a loop is proven: design-for-manufacture, bulk procurement, and data from every project feeding the next. Large funding lets you survive the learning curve that small players can't. You're not Katerra if you scale one product in one region first and use the funding as runway, not as an accelerator **(opinion)**.
*Must be true:* you're willing to say no to projects outside the catalogue.

**4. Outsider: Against as framed · Medium.**
From outside it reads: "We did it late and over budget, so we'll do more of it, in more places." A client doesn't care who owns the factory. They care about one date and one price. Can you show me one building you delivered on that date at that price? If not, why would I sign for the next one?
*Must be true:* you have a reference project.

**5. Executor: Depends · High.**
Tomorrow at 9:00 nobody can say why the first projects slipped. Without that, every new project inherits the same failure. Freeze new commitments, run a root-cause review per project (design changes, factory delays, logistics, site interfaces, permits) and assign a cost and schedule figure to each cause. The blocker is sales pressure to keep winning projects so the factories stay full.
*Must be true:* leadership will tolerate a pause.

**6. Finance: Against scaling now · High.**
A factory is a fixed cost that only works at high, steady utilisation. Construction demand is lumpy and project starts slip. That's Webvan's capacity problem applied to buildings. Multi-project concurrency also ties up working capital in materials and work in progress. Large funding hides bad unit economics until it runs out.
*Must be true:* a contracted pipeline that keeps one factory busy at a known cost per unit.

**7. Technical/Ops: Depends · Medium.**
In my experience, overruns come mostly from design changes after start, tolerance mismatches between factory parts and the site, and sequencing **(opinion, general industry knowledge)**. Integration can fix these only if the design is locked before manufacture. Otherwise you've moved the chaos into a factory, where it costs more.
*Must be true:* a design-freeze gate exists and gets enforced.

### Pre-mortem: 12 months on, it failed. Why?

1. **The factories ran underused while projects slipped**, so fixed costs burned cash. *Early signal:* factory utilisation below plan for 2 months running. Seen in Webvan (capacity far above actual orders).
2. **Design variety killed factory efficiency.** Every project was custom, so there was no repetition. *Early signal:* share of components that are project-specific rather than catalogue items. Seen in Katerra (general knowledge).
3. **Overruns repeated, because their root causes were never fixed** and just moved inside the company. *Early signal:* the next projects show the same causes of delay as the first ones.

### Chair's verdict

**Decision:** Scale a fully integrated construction model across many concurrent projects now, or prove it narrowly first.

**Verdict:** **Don't proceed as planned. Do it like this:** stop taking on new concurrent projects. Find out why the first projects overran. Then prove one standard product in one region with one factory: on time and at or under a conventional build's cost, on several projects in a row. Only then add a product or a region. · Confidence: 8/10

**Agreement:** All seven seats agree the first projects show the system doesn't work yet. They also agree integration only pays off with repetition and a locked design.

**Real disagreement:** Whether integration is the moat or the trap. The council resolves it by sequencing: integrate where you've proven repetition, and buy everything else.

**Assumption that matters most:** "Owning the chain will fix the delays." → **Cheapest test:** a root-cause review of the first projects. If most of the delay and cost overrun came from outside parties you'd replace by owning them, integration is a credible fix. If it came from design changes, site interfaces or internal coordination, ownership won't fix it.

**Closest precedent:** Katerra (verify the details). You're like them in model, funding and early results. You're not them yet, because you haven't scaled. The difference is entirely in what you do next.

**Next step (48 h):** Freeze new project commitments. Assign one owner to produce a one-page root-cause breakdown per completed project: days late and cost overrun, split by design change, factory, supply, site, permits and client.

**Kill criterion:** If, by the end of the next 2–3 projects on a single standard product, delivery isn't within an agreed margin of the contracted date and budget (set the margin now, e.g. ±5%, as an assumption to adjust), don't add regions or factories. Rethink which layers to own, and contract out the rest.
