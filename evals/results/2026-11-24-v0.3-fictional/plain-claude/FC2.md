**Short answer: it's probably a good idea, but your test doesn't show that. A 30% increase for new customers only is common and easy to undo. The weak spot is the evidence, not the plan.**

Here's the council.

---

**The Statistician: "Your test didn't measure anything."**

About 200 visitors means roughly 100 per variant. At a typical 2–5% conversion rate, that's around 2–5 conversions per side. With numbers that small, "conversion didn't change" just means the test couldn't have picked up a change. A drop of 30–40% would look exactly like "no change."

To reliably detect the drop that would actually hurt you (see the CFO below), at a ~3% baseline you'd need about **8,000–9,000 visitors per variant**. You're about 80x short.

---

**The CFO: "The break-even is more forgiving than you think."**

At 1.3x the price, you only lose money if conversion falls by more than **23%** (1 − 1/1.3). So the real question isn't "did conversion stay the same?" It's "did conversion drop by less than about 23%?" For most products with reasonable value, a 30% price change doesn't cut conversion by a quarter. The odds favor you. And if gross margin is high, the extra revenue is almost pure profit.

The metric to track is **revenue per visitor**, not conversion rate.

---

**The Customer Advocate: "Grandfathering is the right call. Make sure it holds up."**

Keeping existing customers on their price removes most of the risk: no churn spike, no angry emails. Watch out for:
- **Discovery.** Existing customers will see the new public price. That usually *helps* ("I've got a good deal"), as long as you don't take it away later without warning.
- **Upgrades and plan changes.** Decide now whether a grandfathered customer who changes plans keeps the old rate. Unclear rules cause support problems.
- **Reactivations.** Does someone who cancels and comes back get the new price? Decide up front.

---

**The Growth Operator: "Conversion isn't the only thing that can break."**

Higher prices can quietly affect metrics further down the funnel that a 200-visitor test would never catch:
- Trial-to-paid rate (if you have trials)
- Refund and early churn (first 30–60 days)
- Mix shift (more people choosing the cheapest plan)
- Sales cycle length and discount requests (if it's B2B)

If any of these move, the 30% can disappear even when top-of-funnel conversion holds.

---

**The Devil's Advocate: "Is 30% the right number, or just a round one?"**

If 30% works, maybe 40% would too. Or maybe 15% gets most of the gain with almost no risk. A single price point tells you nothing about the shape of the demand curve. Also, ask why you think you're underpriced: competitor benchmarks, customer feedback, sales calls where nobody pushed back on price? That evidence is worth more than this A/B test.

---

## Verdict

**Do it, but as a monitored rollout, not a "tested and proven" decision.**

1. **Ship the new price to 100% of new traffic** (or keep a 50/50 split if you have enough volume to finish a proper test within 2–4 weeks).
2. **Set a kill criterion before you start.** For example: "If revenue per visitor is below the old baseline after N conversions, or early churn rises more than X points, we roll back."
3. **Track revenue per visitor, trial-to-paid, 30-day churn and plan mix** for at least one full billing cycle.
4. **Write down the grandfathering rules** (upgrades, downgrades, reactivations) before launch.
5. **Don't announce it as permanent** until the data supports it. For new customers, it's easy to quietly adjust within the first month or two.

The risk is low because you grandfathered existing customers, and the 23% break-even gives you room for error. Just don't describe the 200-visitor test as validation. It's noise.
