## 1. The CFO: "Run the numbers before you argue"

The 40 leads are the strongest fact you have, but they only mean something once you attach money to them:

> **Monthly new revenue left on the table = 40 leads × % qualified × close rate × average deal value**

Two scenarios show how much the answer depends on the inputs:
- **Mid-market deal (€6k ACV)**, 50% qualified, 20% close rate → 4 deals/month → **~€24k in new ACV every month**. A salesperson at €60–80k all-in pays for themselves within a quarter. Hiring is clearly right.
- **Small deal (€50/month)**, same rates → 4 customers → **€200 in new MRR per month**. A salesperson never pays back. Hiring is clearly wrong, and you need automation or a self-serve flow.

With 18 months of runway, one hire probably costs you 2–3 months of runway. That's affordable if the first scenario looks like you. **Your deal size decides this more than anything your co-founder or I could say.**

## 2. The Sales Leader: "You have a leak, not a hiring question"

Inbound leads lose value quickly. A lead that waits a week converts at a fraction of the rate of one you call back within hours. You're paying for those 40 leads through marketing, word of mouth or product effort, and you're throwing most of them away.

A 3-week sales cycle is also a big advantage. A new rep can close their first deals in month 1–2, and **within 90 days you'll know whether the hire works**. Most startups wait 6–9 months to find that out. Your downside is unusually small and quick to spot.

## 3. The Skeptic (your co-founder's case, argued as strongly as I can)

"Too early" is often a sound instinct. The most common way a first sales hire fails looks like this:
- The founders haven't closed enough deals themselves to know **why** people buy, what objections come up, and which leads are worth chasing.
- They hire a salesperson hoping that person will work it out. Without a playbook, the rep struggles, and after 6 months nobody can tell whether the rep, the leads or the product was the problem.
- Meanwhile 6 months of runway are gone.

Some questions to ask honestly: **Have the founders personally closed 10–20 deals? Can you describe your ideal customer and your sales pitch in one page?** If not, the co-founder is right that you aren't ready to hand sales over. That still doesn't justify letting 40 leads a month go cold.

## 4. The Founder Coach: "The real problem is that the leads go unanswered"

You and your co-founder may be arguing about two different things. You're saying "we're losing deals." They may be saying "we don't know how to sell yet," or "I don't want to spend runway on this." **Both of those can be true together.** Ask them directly what exactly feels too early. Is it the money, the playbook, the product, or losing control of the customer relationship? Each one has a different fix.

## 5. The Customer: "I asked, and nobody answered"

From the lead's side, a company that doesn't reply looks either overwhelmed or uninterested. Some of those 40 people are now telling peers they couldn't even get a demo. Ignoring them costs money and also hurts your reputation.

## Verdict: yes, hire, but spend 30 days testing first

On the data you gave me, the case for hiring is strong. The leads and the fast cycle are there, and the runway can cover it. Your co-founder is right about the **order** of steps, though. Here's a plan that addresses both views:

**Days 1–30: Founder sprint**
- One founder blocks 2 hours a day and works **every** new lead within 24 hours.
- Record everything: qualification rate, close rate, deal size, objections, and why people buy.
- Write a one-page playbook covering the ideal customer, discovery questions, demo flow and pricing answers.

**Day 30: Decide using real numbers**
- Plug what you measured into the CFO formula. If one month of closed deals from the backlog covers about 2 months of a salesperson's cost, hire.
- If the numbers are weak, the leads aren't the opportunity you thought. That's worth knowing, and your co-founder was right.

**Days 30–60: Hire carefully**
- Look for a **"builder" closer** who has worked at an early-stage startup and is comfortable without a mature process. Avoid the big-company enterprise rep.
- Set a meaningful variable component (around 50/50 base/commission) and a **90-day milestone** that your short sales cycle makes measurable.
- Hand over the playbook and keep a founder in the weekly pipeline review.

**Stopping point:** if the rep hasn't hit agreed targets after 90 days, stop. You'll have spent about 3 months of salary, not a year.

The decision rule you can both accept: **"We let the leads tell us. 30 days of founder-led sales, then we hire if the numbers clear the bar we agree on today."** That turns a disagreement about opinions into an experiment with a deadline.
