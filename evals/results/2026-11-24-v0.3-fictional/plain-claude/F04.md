**Short answer: not as offered.** The deal could be good, but as described it hands the most leverage to the party with the most conflicts. Here's how the council sees it.

---

### The Optimist (Sales / Growth)
- Biggest player in the sector means instant reach, logistics and credibility in two countries you'd otherwise build out slowly.
- The upfront minimum order is guaranteed cash and lowers the risk of the first year.
- You stop spending on direct sales and can put that money into product or other markets.

### The Skeptic (Strategy)
- **They also carry a competitor.** Ask which brand their salespeople push when margins or incentives favour the other one. The worst version of this is "buy to bury": they lock you up, meet the upfront order, then quietly let your product sit while they protect the incumbent. You can't sell around them for 3 years.
- **The upfront order is a one-time thing. Three years is a long time.** A nice first order tells you nothing about years 2 and 3. If there are no annual minimums, the exclusivity costs them nothing after the first shipment.
- **Stopping direct sales means losing your customers.** You lose the relationships, the data, the pricing feedback and the brand voice. If this ends badly in year 3, you start from zero in Iberia while they hand your old customers to your competitor.

### The Lawyer
- **Leaving can cost you money.** In Spain, courts have sometimes granted distributors a clientele indemnity on termination, by analogy with the Agency Contract Law, if they brought you customers you keep benefiting from. Getting out later may be expensive, so negotiate the exit terms now.
- **Competition law.** Exclusive distribution is generally fine in the EU under the Vertical Block Exemption, but only below roughly 30% market share for each party. "Biggest in the sector" could be above that, which would need a closer look. You also can't fully block passive sales into their territory, and you can't dictate their resale prices.
- Get a local commercial lawyer to review it before signing. This isn't something to handle with a template.

### The CFO
- Do the math against the alternative. Compare their **net price to you × realistic volume** over 3 years with what direct sales would earn you, including the cost of a sales team.
- Check payment terms and returns. A big upfront order on 120-day terms with a right to return unsold stock is a lot less attractive than it looks.

### The Devil's Advocate (for signing)
- If you're small, have no Iberian team and direct sales are weak, a pure "no" might just leave you with nothing. A distributor that knows the market is worth a lot. The real question is the **terms**, not whether to work with them at all.

---

### Verdict: negotiate, then decide
Make these your conditions:

1. **Yearly performance minimums** (volume or revenue), not just the upfront order. If they miss them, the exclusivity automatically drops to non-exclusive, or you can terminate.
2. **A shorter commitment**: 1 year plus renewals based on results, or 3 years with an exit after year 1.
3. **Carve-outs for direct sales**: keep your online store and/or a named list of key accounts.
4. **Conflict safeguards**: a dedicated product manager or sales line for your brand, agreed marketing investment, and quarterly reporting with sell-out data by customer.
5. **Customer data rights**: you get the end-customer data, and it comes back to you when the contract ends.
6. **Exit terms**: notice periods, what happens to stock, and the indemnity capped or settled in advance.
7. **Consider splitting the territories.** Portugal could stay non-exclusive or be a separate deal.

**Watch how they respond.** If they accept performance minimums and an exit clause, they plan to actually sell your product, and the deal can be good. If they push back hard on minimums but insist on exclusivity, they're more interested in controlling your product than selling it. In that case, walk away.
