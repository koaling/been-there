**Decision**: Whether to switch a fast-growing design flash-sale site to owned inventory and buy companies in 5 European countries in the same year.
**Verdict**: Don't do both at once. Do it like this: test inventory on a small slice of proven repeat-selling products in your home market, and enter at most one new country. · Confidence: 7/10

Assumptions, since I have no numbers: you're still loss-making or thin-margin, and the growth comes mostly from paid acquisition plus the novelty of flash sales.

**Agreement**: This is two one-way doors at once. Owning inventory adds working capital, warehouses and markdown risk. Acquisitions add 5 teams, 5 tech stacks and 5 sets of customers to integrate. Doing both multiplies the ways it can fail.
**Real disagreement**: Owning inventory can raise margins *if* you already know what sells again. The Expansionist says investor appetite is a window you should use. The Devil's advocate says it's the same window that funded the precedents below.
**Assumption that matters most**: That your customers come back and buy at full price, not just for the thrill of the sale. If they don't, inventory turns into markdowns. → Cheapest test: pull 12-month repeat purchase rate and contribution margin per order by cohort this week.
**Closest precedent**: Fab.com (from general knowledge, not the library; figures as reported). It was a design flash-sale site that grew very fast, moved to holding its own inventory and expanded in Europe through acquisitions while investors valued it near $1B. Within about two years it went through mass layoffs and was sold for a small fraction of that. You're them unless your repeat and margin data say otherwise.

Precedents
☠ Fab.com — owned inventory plus European acquisitions before the economics worked. Matches all 4 dimensions.
☠ Webvan (1996–2001) — built infrastructure for many markets and bought loss-making HomeGrocer while its first city was still unprofitable. Matches: business model shift, mechanism. Differs: market.
✓ Instacart — beat the same trap by staying asset-light until demand forced it to own things.
✓ Wayfair (general knowledge) — grew in home goods mostly by drop-shipping from suppliers and built its own logistics only gradually.

**Next step (48 h)**: Build a cohort table showing repeat rate, full-price share and contribution margin per order. Pick 20–50 products that have sold in 3 or more sales to pilot as owned stock.
**Kill criterion**: If pilot stock sells less than ~70% at full price within 90 days (estimate, so set your own threshold), or contribution per order hasn't improved by Q2, stop the inventory shift and drop the acquisitions.

▸ Council
- Devil's advocate: Against. This is Fab.com's exact sequence.
- First principles: Your real problem is probably acquisition cost and retention, not margin. Fix that first.
- Expansionist: Depends. Your own inventory could become a design brand, but only built on proven products.
- Outsider: Why would I pay full price at a site I know for sales?
- Executor: Running 5 integrations and a warehouse launch in one year means nobody is watching the core business.
- Finance (seat added for working capital): Inventory turns revenue into cash locked in stock. Model the downside first.
