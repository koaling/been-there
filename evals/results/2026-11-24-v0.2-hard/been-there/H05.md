**Decision**: Raise money to take on-demand pickup-and-ship to 10 cities now, or first prove the economics in your current city.
**Verdict**: First find out whether each order makes money after courier time, then raise. · Confidence: 7/10

You have real pull: NPS 80 and 20% month-over-month growth with no subsidy described. That makes the product worth funding. The open question is the cost behind that love. A €5 fee has to cover a courier who arrives within 20 minutes, packs the item and drops it at a carrier. My estimate: an EU courier costs about €15–20/hour fully loaded, and a pickup, pack and drop-off takes 30–60 minutes. That puts courier cost at roughly €8–20 per order, plus packaging. Unless your carrier markup closes that gap, every order loses money, and 10 cities would multiply the loss.

**Agreement**: Customers clearly want this. What decides it is the cost of each order and how many orders a courier handles per hour, not demand.
**Real disagreement**: The Expansionist reads 20% month-over-month as proof the model works. The Devil's advocate says a below-cost price can also produce that growth.
**Assumption that matters most**: Your margin per order (€5 fee + carrier markup − courier time − packaging − payment fees) is positive today, and orders per courier hour are rising. → Calculate it per order for the last 3 monthly cohorts. Also measure how often the same customer ships again.
**Closest precedent**: Shyp (US, 2013–2018). This one comes from general knowledge, not the library, and I couldn't verify it in this run. It had the same model: on-demand pickup, packing and shipping for about a $5 fee. Customers loved it, it expanded to several US cities, and it shut down in 2018. The founder's shutdown letter said it expanded before the model worked in its first city and later moved to business customers. You're Shyp unless your per-order numbers say otherwise.

▸ Council
- Devil's advocate: A flat fee for a 20-minute courier visit looks like a subsidy. High NPS is normal when something costs less than it should.
- First principles: The problem is "I hate going to the post office." Business customers who ship every day may be a better first customer than households.
- Expansionist: The demand is real. If density makes each order profitable, the playbook can be copied to new cities without building infrastructure, like Instacart.
- Outsider: How often do I actually ship things? Maybe twice a year. How do you keep me?
- Executor: Before writing the pitch deck, pull the order and courier-hour data and calculate the cost per order.
- Finance (it's an economics question): Investors will ask for margin per order and payback on customer acquisition cost. Without them, the round is priced on a growth rate you may be paying for.
- Customer (how often people ship decides retention): Households ship rarely, so acquisition cost has to be recovered over very few orders.
