## The Council: Should you give up?

**Short answer: No. Not on the evidence you've given.** Users coming back every week and inviting colleagues without being asked is what product-market fit looks like early on. Rejection from investors and a giant competitor are real problems, but they're about funding and strategy. They don't show that the product is failing.

Here's how each seat at the table sees it.

---

### 🧭 The Pragmatist (product and metrics)
"Weekly return visits plus organic invites is the hardest signal to fake. Most startups die because nobody cares. Your users care enough to bring in their coworkers. That's the core of a growth loop.

But I need numbers before I'd call it a win. How many beta users? What share are still active at week 4, week 8, week 12? How many invites does each user send, and how many of those people convert? 40 people who love it is a seed. 4,000 is a business."

### 💰 The Investor (the person who keeps saying no)
"I probably didn't pass because the product was bad. I passed because 'simple design tool vs. a giant' makes me ask: why won't the incumbent copy you, or give away a free tier and starve you? If you can't answer that in one sentence, I can't write the check.

Rejections are also often about stage. Without revenue or growth charts, I'm betting on a story. Come back with 6 months of retention and growth curves, and you'll get a different conversation."

### ⚔️ The Strategist (competition)
"Big companies are slow, overbuilt, and made for professionals. That's your opening. Non-designers don't want power. They want to be done in 5 minutes without looking amateur. Don't fight the giant everywhere. Pick a wedge they ignore: one profession (real estate agents, teachers, restaurant owners, HR teams), one workflow, or one channel. Win it completely, then expand.

Keep in mind that a well-known design platform for non-designers was rejected by investors over and over before it raised money. It also competed with a giant that already owned the category. 'A big competitor exists' doesn't predict failure. It's just the market."

### 🔥 The Skeptic (devil's advocate)
"Let me push back. Beta users are often friends, early adopters, or people who don't pay yet. Coming back to a free tool is different from paying for it. And if the invites come from inside the same few companies, your growth might be narrower than it looks.

The real questions: Will anyone pay? How long is your runway? Are you (the founders) burning out? If money runs out in 2 months and nobody has paid a cent, the answer might be 'pivot or pause.' But that's a cash problem, not proof the idea is wrong."

### 🛠️ The Operator (survival without VC)
"You don't need investors to keep going. You need revenue or low burn. Start charging now, even a small amount, for a team plan, brand kits, or export. A product that spreads inside workplaces is perfect for a team/seat model. Ten paying teams teach you more than ten more pitch meetings. And revenue changes the investor conversation completely."

---

### ⚖️ Verdict

| Signal | What it means |
|---|---|
| Weekly retention | 🟢 Strong. People rely on it |
| Organic invites to colleagues | 🟢 Very strong. Built-in distribution |
| Giant competitor | 🟡 Normal. Requires a sharp wedge |
| Dozens of investor rejections | 🟡 A funding/story problem, not a product verdict |
| Unknown: revenue, runway, scale | 🔴 This is what actually decides it |

**Don't give up. Change the question** from "Will investors believe in us?" to "Will users pay us?"

### Next 60–90 days
1. **Measure properly:** cohort retention (W1/W4/W8), invites per user, invite conversion rate.
2. **Charge money:** launch a paid team plan. Even 1–3% conversion proves willingness to pay.
3. **Pick a wedge:** find which user segment retains best and focus everything there.
4. **Cut burn** so runway isn't what forces the decision.
5. **Rewrite the pitch** around data: "X% weekly retention, every user brings Y colleagues, Z teams paying." Then go back to investors, or don't need them.

**When it *would* be time to stop:** retention drops once the novelty wears off, nobody will pay after real attempts, or the team or your finances are breaking. None of these shows up in what you've described so far.

If you share your actual numbers (user count, retention, invites, runway), I can give you a much sharper verdict.
