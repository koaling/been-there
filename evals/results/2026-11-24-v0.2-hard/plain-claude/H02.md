Yes. The council agrees on one thing: **don't do the big national launch next month.** Here's the deliberation.

One piece of history first, because it's very close to your story. In 2011, Color Labs raised about $41M before launch for a proximity-based photo app with no friends list. The team was strong and the tech worked. It launched nationally with a lot of attention, opened to empty feeds and confused users, and was effectively dead within about 18 months. Your plan repeats nearly every part of that one. That doesn't mean the idea can't work. It means the launch plan is the risky part.

---

### The Growth Strategist: "Your product is empty on day one"

Your app only has value when **other users are physically near you at the same moment**. A national launch spreads your users thinly across the whole country. Most people who download it will open it at home, on the bus or at their desk, see nothing (or photos from a stranger in the next building), and never open it again.

- Network effects here are **hyper-local and simultaneous**. A million users across a country can still mean zero useful density anywhere.
- Paid acquisition and press make this worse. They bring in a huge wave of people who all hit the empty room at once, and you only get one first impression.
- **Change:** launch where density is guaranteed. Pick one university campus, one festival, a few weddings or conferences, or a stadium. Win one place, then the next.

### The Product Lead: "Automatic sharing with strangers isn't what people want"

"No friends list" sounds like less friction. In practice it means **no context**. People share photos *with someone* and *for a reason*. Being near someone is a weak proxy for that.

- Who is near you is usually strangers: the café, the gym, the office floor below.
- The moments where being nearby really does signal a shared experience are events: a concert, a wedding, a match, a trip, a party. That's your real product.
- **Change:** reframe it as **instant shared albums for the people you're with right now.** Make it session-based and opt-in ("Start a moment"), with a time limit, and let people easily keep or leave the group afterwards. That way your proximity tech removes the friction of setting up a group without removing user intent.

### The Trust & Safety / Legal Officer: "This is a liability as currently designed"

Automatically sending photos to whoever is nearby has serious problems, especially in Europe:

- **GDPR:** photos contain other people's faces, and precise location is personal data. Automatic sharing without clear, specific consent per share is very hard to defend. Regulators will look closely at a high-profile launch.
- **Safety:** someone can stand near a person and receive their photos. That enables stalking, revealing someone's location, and the targeting of minors. Expect a news story about this within weeks.
- **Content:** unsolicited explicit images get pushed to strangers automatically ("AirDrop flashing" at scale), plus a risk of illegal content. The DSA requires notice-and-action, reporting and risk assessments.
- **Minors:** age assurance plus proximity to strangers is a red-flag combination for regulators and app stores.
- **Change:** no automatic sharing to strangers by default, explicit opt-in, a preview and confirm step before anything is sent, moderation before content is shown to others, blocking and reporting, age gating, and a DPIA done *before* launch, not after.

### The CFO / Board Member: "Money isn't your constraint. Learning is."

€40M feels like runway, but it tends to buy a big launch, a big team and big burn before product-market fit exists. A national launch also makes your failure very public, which hurts the next pivot and the next raise.

- **Change:** treat the next 3 to 6 months as **controlled experiments**. Set clear gates before you scale: for example, D7/D30 retention in a test location, the share of sessions where 2+ users are actually in range, and photos viewed per session. Only spend on growth once one location retains users without paid push.

### The Contrarian: "What if the tech is the product, not the app?"

If the proximity technology really is excellent, there may be a better business in **B2B or embedded use**: event organisers, venues, wedding photographers, sports clubs and festivals that want a "shared album of tonight" for attendees. These customers *bring the density* with them and pay for it. Keep this as a parallel option rather than betting everything on consumer virality.

---

### Council verdict

| Area | Current plan | Recommended change |
|---|---|---|
| Launch | Big national launch next month | Postpone. Run a dense pilot in 1 to 3 contexts (campus, festival, events) |
| Core mechanic | Auto-share with anyone nearby | Opt-in, time-limited "moments" with people you're actually with |
| Positioning | Social network without friends | Instant shared album for an event or group |
| Safety/legal | (unclear) | DPIA, consent per share, moderation, age gating, blocking, before any public launch |
| Spend | Scale marketing | Fund experiments with clear retention gates, and keep most of the €40M |
| Optionality | Consumer only | Test B2B event/venue partnerships in parallel |

**The one-sentence answer:** a great team and great tech don't fix a cold-start problem or a consent problem. Shrink the launch until density is guaranteed, make sharing a choice, and scale only what retains.
