Here's the council. Five seats, each asked the same thing: should you raise to go to 10 cities?

---

### 1. The CFO: "Show me the cost of one pickup"

The numbers you gave are about demand. None of them are about cost, and in this model cost decides everything.

Rough cost of one pickup in a Spanish city (illustrative, plug in your real numbers):

| Cost line | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Courier time (travel + wait + pack + drop-off, ~35–45 min at a loaded €16–20/h) | €10–14 |
| Packaging materials (box, filler, tape, label) | €1–3 |
| Idle capacity needed to hold a 20-min SLA | +20–40% on labour |
| Payment fees, support, damage claims | €0.5–1.5 |
| **Total cost per pickup** | **~€13–20** |
| **Revenue per pickup** | **€5 + whatever margin you take on carrier rates** |

Unless you're making €8–15 per parcel on carrier margin (unlikely, since customers can see carrier prices), **every order loses money.** If that's true, 20% month-over-month growth means losses also grow 20% a month, and 10 cities means 10 times the losses.

**The question:** what is your contribution margin per order today, after courier labour and packaging? If you don't know it to the euro, that's the first job.

---

### 2. The historian: "This has been tried, and the pattern is familiar"

Shyp (US, 2013–2018) is almost exactly this business: on-demand pickup, they pack it, they ship it, with a small flat fee. It had great reviews, fast growth and roughly $60M+ raised, and it shut down. The usual post-mortem says:

- Customers loved it **because it was subsidised**. The flat fee was far below the real cost.
- Expanding to new cities multiplied the losses before any one city worked.
- When they raised the price to cover costs, demand dropped sharply.
- Most users ship rarely (a few times a year), so retention and frequency never justified the acquisition cost.

Others followed a similar path (some pivoted to B2B, others closed). That doesn't mean you'll fail. It does mean any good investor will ask "why aren't you Shyp?" in the first meeting, and you need an answer backed by data.

---

### 3. The investor: "NPS 80 and 20% MoM aren't what I'll underwrite"

What a serious VC will check, in this order:

1. **Unit economics per order and per city.** Is even one city contribution-positive?
2. **Cohort retention and frequency.** How many orders per customer in months 1, 3 and 6? Shipping is infrequent for consumers, and that's the core risk.
3. **Where growth comes from.** Is it organic or word of mouth, or paid and promo-driven? 20% MoM on a small base (say 400 to 480 orders) shows less than it sounds.
4. **Price elasticity.** Have you tested €9, €12 or €15? An NPS of 80 at a loss-making price says nothing about willingness to pay at a break-even price.
5. **Density curve.** Does cost per pickup actually fall as orders per km² rise? If you have the data, show it. It's the whole thesis.

If you can show one city where cost per order falls with density and reaches positive contribution margin at a price people still pay, you have a fundable story. Without that, you're asking for money to scale a loss.

---

### 4. The operator: "The 20-minute promise is your most expensive feature"

- **Do customers need 20 minutes?** Shipping is rarely urgent. A 2-hour window, or "tonight between 19:00 and 21:00", lets you batch 5–8 pickups into one route. That can cut cost per pickup by more than half.
- **Packing at the door** is slow and has a lot of variance (a vase vs. a book). Consider picking up unpacked items and packing them centrally at a hub. That's faster for the courier, gives more consistent quality and allows cheaper bulk materials.
- **Carrier rates:** with volume you can negotiate rates well below retail. That spread can become your real margin, which is the model that tends to work in this space.
- **Liability:** you're packing other people's things. Breakage claims, prohibited items (batteries, liquids) and insurance all need a clear policy before you multiply volume by 10.

---

### 5. The optimist: "There is a real business in here somewhere"

The demand is real. People hate going to the post office, and an NPS of 80 is hard to fake. The question is which segment pays enough:

- **Small online sellers (Vinted, Wallapop, Etsy, small e-commerce):** they ship weekly or daily, with high frequency and predictable volume. A scheduled daily pickup plus packing for a monthly subscription could have very different economics.
- **Returns:** retailers already pay for reverse logistics. White-label home-pickup returns for brands is a B2B contract, not a consumer acquiring cost.
- **Premium consumer:** keep "20 minutes" as a priced express option (€12–15) and make the scheduled window the cheap default.

---

## Verdict of the council

**Don't raise to expand to 10 cities yet.** Raise, or bridge, to **prove one city.** Expansion multiplies whatever the unit economics are, and right now that's probably a loss.

Before any expansion round, have these on one slide:

1. **Contribution margin per order** in your current city, fully loaded.
2. **Cohort retention:** orders per customer over 6 months.
3. **Price test results:** demand at €5 vs. €9 vs. €12+, and scheduled vs. 20-minute pickup.
4. **Density curve:** cost per pickup versus orders per zone per day.
5. **Segment split:** what share of orders comes from repeat sellers vs. one-off consumers, and the margin on each.

If those come back good, the 10-city raise becomes easy to justify and much easier to close. If they don't, you've found out before spending someone else's money finding out in 10 cities at once.
