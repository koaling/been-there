**Short verdict: don't go big yet.** "The tech works" and "the business works" are separate claims, and you've only shown the first. This exact model has been tried already. Zume Pizza raised several hundred million dollars, much of it from SoftBank, to build robot kitchens and ovens inside delivery trucks. It dropped pizza around 2020 and later shut down. Before you buy a fleet, you need a clear answer to why your version is different.

Here's the council.

---

### 1. The CFO: "Show me the unit economics in one city first."
- Each truck is a commercial kitchen on wheels. You pay for the vehicle, the ovens, power, maintenance, insurance, a driver, and the time it sits idle between rushes. Compare that with a scooter carrying a hot bag.
- Pizza is a low-ticket, low-margin, very competitive product. The extra capital per order has to come back through a higher price, more orders, or lower labor costs. Which one is it, and by how much?
- **What I need before any expansion:** positive contribution margin per order, *including truck depreciation*, in at least one market at steady state, not launch-week numbers.

### 2. The Customer: "Do I actually notice the difference?"
- Domino's and the local places already get a decent pizza to me in about 30 minutes. A 10–20 minute hot-bag trip doesn't ruin a pizza.
- Freshness only wins if customers taste it, **pay more for it, and reorder more often**. Novelty gets you a first order. Retention is the real test.
- **Test:** blind taste tests against competitors, plus cohort retention at 30, 60 and 90 days compared with category benchmarks.

### 3. The Operator: "The truck is the bottleneck."
- Cooking on the move only works if the truck knows what to bake before it reaches the customer. That means batching orders and predicting demand along routes, and both are hard at low order density.
- At peak (Friday night), the number of trucks caps your throughput. Off-peak, those expensive assets sit idle.
- Breakdowns, traffic, and oven faults while driving put a whole truckload of orders at risk at once, not just one.
- **Question:** how many orders per truck per hour can you do, peak and off-peak, and what's the utilization across a whole week?

### 4. The Regulator / Legal: "Every city is a new permit fight."
- Mobile food units, cooking in a moving vehicle, fire safety (ovens, gas or high-power electric), food handling, parking and idling rules. These vary by city and sometimes by district.
- Expanding to several cities at once multiplies compliance work and the risk of delays.
- **Ask:** how long did permitting take in your first city, and can that process be repeated?

### 5. The Strategist: "What are you actually the best at?"
- Your real asset might be the **robotic kitchen and routing software**, not the pizza brand. Zume ended up pivoting toward sustainable packaging, which suggests the team saw its value somewhere other than pizza.
- Other options to consider: licensing the kitchen automation to existing chains, using fixed robot kitchens close to demand instead of trucks, or moving to higher-margin food categories where freshness matters more.
- **Question:** if a competitor copied your trucks tomorrow, what would still protect you?

### 6. The Optimist: "There is a real case for going big."
- Delivery is a density game. Being first in a city can lock in brand, routes and data. A large round gives you room to buy that position before competitors arrive.
- If your numbers in the first city are *already* strong, waiting too long has its own cost.
- **The condition:** this only holds if the economics of one city are proven. Expanding at scale before that just burns cash faster.

---

### Recommendation: stage-gated expansion, not a big launch

1. **Prove it in one city, completely:** reach positive contribution margin per order including truck costs, hit a target truck utilization, and show 90-day retention above the category benchmark.
2. **Then a second city** to test repeatability: launch cost, time to permits, and time to break even. Avoid launching in several cities at once.
3. **Lease or modularize trucks** so you aren't locked into a big fleet you can't redeploy.
4. **Protect your runway.** A large round is what lets you survive mistakes. Don't spend it all on vehicles before the model is validated.
5. **Look at alternatives in parallel**, such as fixed robot kitchens, B2B licensing and other food categories, so that if the trucks don't pay off you have somewhere to go.

**What would change my answer to "go big":** strong per-order economics in your current city, above-benchmark repeat rates, and a clear explanation of what you're doing differently from Zume. If you have those, share the numbers and I'll help you pressure-test the expansion plan.
