**Short answer: no. You're treating growth and creator pay as separate things, but they're connected. Your top creators are a big part of why you're growing. When they ask for a revenue share, they're telling you they might leave.**

Here's how a few different advisors would see it:

---

### 1. The Growth Lead
"Focus on growth" is fair, but ask what's actually driving it. On short-video apps, a small group of top creators makes most of the content people watch. If a handful of them start posting first on a competitor, your growth curve can bend in a matter of weeks, not quarters. Keeping those creators *is* a growth task.

### 2. The Historian
Vine already went through this. Around 2015, a group of its biggest creators reportedly asked to be paid for continuing to post. Vine said no, they moved to YouTube and Instagram, and their audiences went with them. Vine was shut down soon after. Being the most popular app with creators doesn't protect you. Their loyalty lasts only until someone else pays them.

### 3. The Competitor Strategist
Your rivals are much bigger and already pay creators through revenue shares, creator funds and bonuses. A well-funded competitor could easily recruit your top 20 creators with guaranteed deals. Right now your best creators are your weakest point, and you're waiting for someone else to notice.

### 4. The CFO
Waiting has two costs:
- **Creator expectations harden.** If you start paying only once you're big, creators will compare you to established platforms, and the first offer you make will look small.
- **You know nothing about your unit economics.** You don't know what an engaged teen viewer is worth in ads, so you can't tell whether growth at this scale can ever pay for itself. Investors will ask.

A small creator pilot doesn't commit you to much, but it gets you real data.

### 5. The Trust & Safety / Legal Lead (the advisor you most need to hear)
Your audience is teens, and probably many of your creators are too. That's why designing monetisation late is dangerous:
- **Paying minors** means parental consent, identity checks, tax handling and possibly local child-performer rules. That plumbing takes time to build.
- **Making money from a teen audience** is heavily regulated. In the EU, the DSA bans ads based on profiling for minors. You also have COPPA in the US (under 13), the UK Children's Code, and age-assurance requirements spreading across several countries.
- If you design monetisation *after* you're huge, you'll do it in a rush, under investor pressure and with regulators watching. That's how platforms end up in headlines. Doing it early and deliberately is much safer.

### 6. The Team Lead (taking your side)
Your worry about distraction is real. A full monetisation system with a creator dashboard, ad stack and automated payouts *would* pull the team off growth for months. Don't build that yet.

---

### Council verdict

**Right instinct, wrong conclusion.** Don't build full monetisation now, but don't make your top creators wait either. Do something light now and save the heavy build for later:

1. **This month: talk to your top 20–50 creators directly.** Find out what they actually want. It may be money, but it may also be visibility, brand deals, tools or early access to features.
2. **Next 1–2 months: run a small, mostly manual pilot.** Offer a modest fund or direct contracts to top creators, paid through a payments provider or agency, with almost no engineering. One partnerships person can run it, so the product team isn't pulled in.
3. **Start now: shape monetisation around minors.** Decide whether you'll pay under-18s and how, and which ad models are legal for your audience in each market. Legal and policy work can run alongside the team without taking their time.
4. **Later: build the full system,** once the pilot shows what creators value and what a viewer is worth.

**The real question:** if your three biggest creators announced tomorrow that they're moving to a competitor, what would happen to your growth numbers? If the answer scares you, this is already a priority.
