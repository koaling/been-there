## The Council

**The CFO**
Holding inventory turns you from an asset-light marketplace into a retailer that needs a lot of cash. Right now suppliers probably carry the stock risk and you collect cash before you pay. Once you own inventory, cash goes out months before it comes back, and every SKU that doesn't sell becomes a markdown or a write-off. Design products sell like fashion. They're trend-driven and hard to forecast, and they go stale. Higher gross margin on paper can easily turn into lower margin once you count unsold stock, warehousing and markdowns. Before anything else I'd want to see contribution margin per order *after* those costs, not before.

**The Head of Operations**
Faster delivery is a real customer benefit, but it also means warehouses, a WMS, demand planning, a returns flow, and the people to run all of it. Now multiply that by five countries, each with its own carriers, language, customer service, VAT and EU returns rules (14-day withdrawal right, and returns of bulky design items are expensive). You'd be building an operations company and integrating acquisitions at the same moment. Either one is hard enough. Doing both at once is how execution breaks.

**The Customer and Growth Lead**
Why did you grow so fast? Flash-sale growth is often driven by novelty, discounts and heavy acquisition spend rather than loyalty. If the core engine isn't proven, you'll be pouring fuel on it. I'd want cohort data: are customers from 12 to 18 months ago still buying? What's the repeat rate, and how long does CAC take to pay back? If those numbers are weak, inventory and new countries won't fix them. They'll make the burn bigger.

**The M&A Skeptic**
Five acquisitions in one year means five tech stacks, five cultures, five sets of founders whose motivation changes once they've been paid, and five brands to migrate or keep. Most of the value you're "buying" is the customer list, and it churns quickly if the experience changes during integration. Ask whether you're buying capability or buying a growth line for the next board deck.

**The Investor-Relations Realist**
Investors being excited is information about investors, not about the plan. Big, bold moves raise big rounds, and big rounds create pressure to deploy capital fast. That pressure is a risk in itself.

**The Historian**
This has almost exactly happened before. Fab.com was a fast-growing design flash-sale site. Around 2012–2013 it moved toward holding its own inventory, expanded across Europe by acquiring local players, raised hundreds of millions, and reached a valuation near $1B. Within about two years it had collapsed and was sold for a small fraction of that. The direction wasn't absurd. The problem was doing several capital-heavy changes at once before the core economics were proven.

---

## Synthesis: Not as one combined move this year

Each idea could make sense on its own, with evidence and in stages. Together, at speed, you'd be stacking three risks: working capital, operational build-out, and integration. And all of it would rest on a growth engine whose durability you haven't shown yet.

**What I'd do instead:**

1. **Prove the core first.** Look at cohort retention, repeat purchase rate, CAC payback and contribution margin per order. If they're healthy, you have earned the right to scale. If not, fix that first.
2. **Pilot inventory, don't switch to it.** Use a hybrid model. Buy stock only for your proven bestsellers (say the top 10–20% by sell-through) and keep everything else on drop-ship or consignment. Set clear targets for sell-through, inventory turns and markdown rate, and expand only when you hit them.
3. **Expand to one country, not five.** Pick the market that looks most like your best existing one. Try organic entry or a single small acquisition first, and learn what localization and logistics really cost.
4. **Set kill criteria in advance.** Decide now which numbers would make you stop or slow down (for example cash runway below X months, or sell-through below Y%), so the decision doesn't get made under pressure later.
5. **Raise for runway, not for speed.** If you raise on investor enthusiasm, use the money to extend your margin for error, not to compress the timeline.

**Questions to answer before deciding:**
- What share of revenue comes from repeat customers?
- What do margins look like *after* inventory risk, warehousing and returns?
- How many months of runway would you have if sell-through came in 30% below plan?
- Do you have anyone on the team who has run inventory retail or post-merger integration?

**Bottom line:** the direction may be right, but the pace and the combination are dangerous. Do these moves in order, one at a time, and let the data decide each next step rather than investor excitement.
