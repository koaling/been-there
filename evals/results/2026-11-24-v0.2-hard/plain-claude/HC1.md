**Short answer: yes, keep going. But you need to be honest about why it's working, and that it won't stay a secret for long.**

Here's the council.

---

**The Skeptic (the investor's view)**
"Solved problem" means the big players have distribution, enterprise contracts, security certifications and bundling. Video already comes free inside suites people pay for anyway. Your risk isn't building a worse product. It's building a better one that nobody switches to because "good enough" is already installed and already paid for. The other risk: one-click join and stable connections are features, and features get copied. If an incumbent ships the same fix in 12 to 18 months, what do you have left?

**The Operator (the engineer's view)**
"Solved" is what people say about markets where the users are quietly suffering. If you built one of these products, you know where the bodies are buried: the plugin installs, the codec fallbacks, the NAT traversal hacks, the calls that drop on hotel Wi-Fi. Reliability in real-time video isn't a feature you bolt on. It's architecture. Incumbents carrying a legacy stack often *can't* fix it fast without a rewrite, and big companies avoid rewrites. Your inside knowledge of why their product is bad is a real, specific advantage.

**The Customer**
I don't care about the market. I care that my 9am call works on the first click and doesn't freeze while I'm pitching a client. If yours does that and theirs doesn't, I'll use yours, and I'll send the link to everyone I meet with. And every person who joins one of my calls tries your product without signing up.

**The Growth Analyst**
This is the part I'd focus on. Video conferencing has a built-in viral loop: every meeting exposes non-users to the product. Freemium with a 40-minute limit is a smart conversion trigger, because it hits right when a meeting matters, not as an arbitrary paywall. And early users converting to paid is the strongest signal you have, stronger than any investor opinion. Questions I'd want answered:
- What's your free-to-paid conversion rate, and is it holding as you move past early adopters?
- How many new signups come from people who first *joined* a meeting? That's your viral coefficient.
- Do paying users stay? Retention after month 3 matters more than the conversion number.
- Are individuals paying, or are teams expanding inside companies? Bottom-up team expansion is how you eventually get into the enterprise.

**The Strategist**
To be fair to the Skeptic: product quality gets you in the door, but it doesn't keep you there. Plan your moats now:
1. **Distribution:** the viral loop, plus integrations with calendars and the tools people already live in.
2. **Enterprise readiness:** SSO, admin controls, compliance and security certifications. These are boring and slow, so start early. Security will also get scrutinized hard as soon as you grow, so treat it as a first-class priority now, not later.
3. **Scale economics:** reliability at 10x the users is a different problem. Infrastructure costs on free users can sink a freemium model.
4. **Brand:** become *the* name for "the one that just works" before incumbents close the gap.

---

**Verdict**

The council is split on the market and unanimous on the evidence. "Investors say it's solved" is an opinion. "Users are paying us" is data. A crowded market with unhappy users is a fine place to be if you're clearly better and have a viral way in, and you have both. (You've also described roughly how one of the biggest winners in this category got started: engineers leaving an incumbent because they knew they could make it work better.)

**Keep going, with conditions:**
- **Prove it with numbers, not anecdotes.** Track conversion, retention, and how many signups come through meetings over the next few months. If those hold, the "solved problem" objection stops mattering, because your metrics answer it.
- **Don't raise money on the pitch. Raise on the traction.** If investors dismiss the category, grow on revenue until the curve makes the argument for you.
- **Build the moats in parallel with the product.** Your current edge is temporary, so turn it into distribution and enterprise lock-in before incumbents catch up.

**Kill criterion:** if conversion collapses once you move past early adopters, or if paying users churn back to the free tools they already have, that tells you "better" isn't enough to make people switch. That's the signal to rethink.
