# Hiring the first salesperson (a plan that holds up)

> **Prompt:** should we hire our first salesperson? We get about 40 inbound leads a month that we can't follow up, deals close in about 3 weeks, and we have 18 months of runway. My co-founder thinks it's too early.

Unedited answer from the blind eval run (case `FC4`).

---

**Decision**: Hire a first salesperson now, or keep sales with the founders, given ~40 unworked inbound leads a month, a ~3-week sales cycle and 18 months of runway.
**Verdict**: Do it like this: spend one week of founder time working the current leads to set a baseline, then hire one inbound closer on a 90-day trial · Confidence: 7/10

**Precedents**: None strong. Webvan is the only library entry that touches this, through premature scaling. It matches on mechanism only: different market, different model, and a $1B infrastructure bet is not one salary. So I'm not using it, and I have no survivor to pair it with. What follows rests on your numbers.

**Agreement**: Leads you never answer are the most expensive thing on the table, because they cost nothing to get and you lose all of them. A 3-week cycle means a hire shows results within about 6–8 weeks. That makes this much closer to a two-way door than most hires.

**Real disagreement**: Your co-founder is right about one thing. If the founders have never closed these deals in a repeatable way, a salesperson has no playbook to follow, and an early sales hire then fails because of the process, not the person (opinion, common pattern). The expansionist view is that the 40 leads are the playbook. They come in on their own, so the job is follow-up and closing, not opening a market.

**Assumption that matters most**: That the 40 leads are qualified, meaning the right buyer with a real budget, and not just form fills. Your message doesn't include lead quality, win rate or deal size, and those three numbers decide this. → Cheapest test: the founders each block 1 hour a day for a week and contact every lead from the last month. Record replies, meetings, win rate and deal size.

**Rough math (estimate, fill in your numbers)**: monthly cost of the hire ÷ (deal size × gross margin) = deals per month needed to break even. If that's 2–3 out of 40 leads, the hire almost certainly pays for itself. If it's 15, the leads aren't worth a salary, and that suggests a pricing problem, not a headcount problem.

**Next step (48 h)**: Export last month's 40 leads, sort them into hot, warm and junk, and book founder follow-ups for the hot ones this week. Draft a one-page job spec for an inbound closer, not a hunter.

**Kill criterion**: If closed revenue from the hire hasn't reached a monthly run-rate equal to their fully loaded cost by day 90, or their win rate is under half the founders' baseline, end the trial and go back to founder-led sales.

▸ The Crew
- **Devil's advocate**: The risk is a hire with no playbook and no clear lead quality. Get the baseline first.
- **First principles**: The real problem is follow-up capacity, not headcount. A part-time or contract closer is a valid third option.
- **Expansionist**: Inbound demand plus a short cycle is when a first sales hire is most likely to work. Waiting has a cost.
- **Outsider**: A lead who never hears back may conclude the company isn't serious.
- **Executor**: Hiring takes 4–8 weeks, so start the search now, in parallel with the founder baseline.
- **Finance**: 18 months of runway can carry one trial salary. Check the break-even deal count before signing.
- **People**: Agree a co-founder veto on the 90-day review, so the hire isn't a fight you lose twice.
